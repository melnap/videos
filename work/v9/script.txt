Not everything at Dollar Tree is a bargain, but some of it really is. A few Dollar Tree foods are worth keeping in your pantry all winter.

You walk in thinking everything costs a dollar. It doesn't anymore. Many items that used to be a dollar twenty-five now cost a dollar fifty, and the store keeps adding things at three and five dollars. The price tags still look small, so it's easy to fill a basket and walk out believing you saved money, when some of those packages cost more per ounce than the same food at a big grocery store.

That isn't your fault. A small number on a shelf tag is designed to feel like a deal. The only way to know is to look past the price at what you're actually getting.

So here are twelve Dollar Tree foods worth stocking before winter, the ones that really are good value, keep well on a pantry shelf, and help on the snowy days when you'd rather not go out. One is a box that costs well under a dollar and turns into a whole pan of bread. One jar near the honey isn't really honey. And the one at number twelve works out to about thirty cents a meal. At the end, five things I never buy there, no matter how small the price looks.

First, the rule that makes the whole trip work. Read the size, not just the price. Dollar Tree packages are often smaller than the ones at the grocery store. The same money for less food has a name, shrinkflation, and it's the first thing to watch for. A dollar fifty for eight ounces is a very different deal from a dollar fifty for twenty-four. Look for the unit price, the price per ounce. When a shelf tag doesn't show it, do the math on your phone. It takes ten seconds, and it's the difference between saving money and just spending less at a time.

And as with any food storage, check the best-by date on everything before it goes in the basket. Dollar stores sell a lot of closeouts, and some boxes are closer to their date than you'd expect.

Number one is ground black pepper and the basic spices. This is one of the clearest wins in the store. In March of this year, MoneyLion compared a Dollar Tree tin of ground black pepper at a dollar twenty-five with a smaller jar of a name brand at Walmart for about two and a half dollars. Pepper is pepper, and most shoppers can't taste the difference. Ground spices keep their best flavor for two to three years in a closed container, according to North Dakota State University Extension, so this is an easy place to fill the gaps in your spice rack before holiday cooking starts. Keep them away from the stove, because heat and steam fade them faster.

Number two is dry pasta. Many boxes are a dollar fifty, and pasta is the definition of a pantry food that keeps. North Dakota State gives dry pasta two years unopened and about a year once the box is open, as long as you move it into an airtight container. A box of spaghetti, a jar of sauce or a can of tomatoes, and whatever is in the freezer is dinner for four. Just check the weight on the box. Some boxes are a full pound, and some are smaller. And if you've ever opened a box and found tiny bugs, here's the fix. University of Minnesota Extension says that keeping dry foods at zero degrees for at least four days kills pantry insects and their eggs. So new boxes of pasta can spend a few days in the freezer before they go on the shelf.

Number three is a boxed spaghetti dinner kit, the kind with pasta, a seasoning packet and grated cheese. The Daily Meal listed Kraft's Spaghetti Classics at Dollar Tree for a dollar fifty in July. It's an eight-ounce box that serves four, and all you add is tomato sauce or paste. That's a full dinner on the shelf for the night the roads are icy and nobody wants to leave the house. Keep it with your pasta and use the oldest box first.

Number four is boxed pasta and rice side dishes. These are the quick sides you stir into boiling water, and Dollar Tree carries name brands for a dollar fifty. They're a good fit for busy weeknights in winter, when a pound of chicken and one box turn into a full meal. One thing to know: the rice mixes don't keep as long as plain rice. North Dakota State gives flavored rice mixes about six months, because of the seasoning and oil in the packet. So buy a few, not a case.

Number five is the little box of cornbread mix. Tasting Table found Jiffy cornbread mix at Dollar Tree for seventy-five cents in June. That small box, an egg and a little milk make a whole pan of cornbread, which is about as close to free bread as a pantry gets. In winter it goes next to every pot of chili and soup, and it's half of a Thanksgiving dressing. Cornbread and muffin mixes keep about nine months unopened, according to North Dakota State. Buy what you'll bake by spring.

Number six is cake and brownie mixes. Pillsbury yellow and chocolate cake mixes were a dollar fifty at Dollar Tree this summer, according to Tasting Table. With Thanksgiving on November twenty-sixth and the holidays right after, a few boxes on the shelf mean a birthday cake, a church potluck or a last-minute dessert never needs a special trip. Cake mix keeps twelve to eighteen months unopened. Brownie mix is shorter, about nine months, so don't overbuy that one. And here's an old trick worth knowing. A box of spice cake mix stirred together with a can of pumpkin and baked makes moist pumpkin muffins with no eggs and no oil. Two items, one bowl, and a whole tray for the bake sale.

Number seven is the small bottle of vegetable oil. This one surprises people. Tasting Table found a sixteen-ounce bottle at Dollar Tree for a dollar fifty, compared with three dollars and forty-nine cents for the same size at the author's local grocery store. And here's why the small bottle is actually the smart size. Cooking oil doesn't keep long. North Dakota State gives an unopened bottle about six months, and an opened one three to five. A huge jug often goes stale before you finish it. Two small bottles, used one at a time, stay fresh.

Number eight is pickles. Most jars were about a dollar fifty in June, in everything from bread and butter chips to spicy spears. Pickles make plain meals feel like more: a sandwich, a plate of beans, a bowl of potato salad. And don't pour the juice down the drain. It works in salad dressing, in a marinade for chicken, and in deviled eggs. Once a jar is open, keep it in the refrigerator and use it within one to three months, according to North Dakota State.

Number nine is jarred olives. Tasting Table found pitted kalamata olives for a dollar fifty, compared with two forty-nine at a regular grocery store. A jar of olives turns plain pasta, a pan of roasted potatoes or a simple salad into something that feels special, and that matters in a long winter of pantry cooking. The same rule applies as for pickles: one to three months in the refrigerator after you open the jar.

Number ten is condiments, ketchup and mustard. MoneyLion found a large bottle of store-brand ketchup, around twenty-four ounces, for a dollar fifty. The fourteen-ounce name brand at Target was about three seventy-nine. FinanceBuzz listed a twelve-ounce mustard for a dollar fifty in September. Unopened ketchup keeps about a year in the pantry, and prepared yellow mustard keeps one to two years, according to North Dakota State. Once ketchup is open, the refrigerator keeps it good longer.

Number eleven is breakfast cereal. The Daily Meal found Nature Valley cereal at Dollar Tree for a dollar fifty in July, and name-brand cereal is one of the places the savings can be real. But this is exactly where you check the ounces, because boxes vary a lot in size. Ready-to-eat cereal keeps six to twelve months unopened, and two to three months once the bag is open, according to North Dakota State. Fold the inner bag down tight, or move it into a container, and it stays crisp.

And number twelve is instant ramen. MoneyLion found a five-pack of Top Ramen at Dollar Tree for a dollar fifty in March, about thirty cents a pack. The six-pack at Target cost two ninety-nine. Thirty cents a meal is hard to beat. But ramen on its own isn't much of a dinner, so here's how to make it one. Crack an egg into the pot for the last two minutes. Add a handful of frozen vegetables or the leftover chicken from Sunday. Use half the seasoning packet, which cuts the salt, and add a spoonful of peanut butter or a splash of soy sauce instead. Now it's a warm bowl that costs under a dollar and fills you up on a cold night. Keep the packs in a closed container so they stay crisp, and check the printed date.

Now the five things I never buy at Dollar Tree.

The first is anything sold as a honey blend or honey syrup. It sits right next to the honey and looks like honey, but it's a mixture, not pure honey. Read the front label and the ingredients. If you want honey, buy pure honey, which keeps for years.

The second is plain canned vegetables. Tasting Table reported in July that Walmart sold canned corn for a little over sixty cents a can, while Dollar Tree often charged about a dollar. Plain store-brand canned vegetables are almost always cheaper at a big grocery store, at Aldi, or on sale.

The third is the cheese. Some of it is labeled as a pasteurized process cheese product or topping rather than cheese, and reviewers say it doesn't melt well. It also needs the refrigerator, so it does nothing for your pantry.

The fourth is frozen steak. Reviewers describe the cuts as thin and pale. When meat is part of your winter plan, buy it where you can see it, on sale, and freeze it yourself.

And the fifth is the small snack bags. Many are only about two ounces for a dollar twenty-five. That's one of the most expensive ways to buy chips by the ounce. If your family wants chips, the big bag at the grocery store is cheaper.

So that's the list. Twelve foods that really are good value at Dollar Tree right now, and five that only look like it.

Here's what those twelve look like on a snowy weekend, when nobody wants to drive. Saturday lunch is ramen with an egg and frozen vegetables. Saturday dinner is the spaghetti kit with a jar of olives on the side. Sunday there's a pot of chili or soup with a pan of cornbread, and pickles on the table. And if the storm turns into a birthday or a church potluck, there's a cake mix waiting in the cupboard. That's a whole weekend of meals from a shelf you filled for about twenty dollars, and not one of those boxes needs the refrigerator until you open it.

Before you go, one habit makes all of this work. Take a short list with you, and only buy from the list. A dollar store is full of small impulse buys, and the low price on each item makes it easy to spend twenty dollars on things you didn't plan to buy. A list keeps the savings real.

And when you get home, put the new boxes and jars behind the older ones in your pantry, so the oldest gets used first. Prices and selection vary from store to store, so check your own Dollar Tree, but the rule never changes. Read the size, check the date, and only keep what your family will actually eat. Do that, and a few short trips this fall can carry your pantry through the whole winter.
