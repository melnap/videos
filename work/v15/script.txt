The baking staples in your cupboard right now could ruin your holiday baking.

Not because you bought the wrong brand. Because every one of them has a clock, and nobody told you when it started.

You know how it goes. The cookies spread flat across the pan. The cinnamon rolls sit there and never rise. The pumpkin pie tastes like dust instead of spice. You followed the recipe exactly, so you blame yourself, or the oven. But most of the time, the recipe was fine. One ingredient had quietly stopped working, and the date on the box never warned you.

So today I'm going through the twelve baking staples I stock up on before the holidays, how long each one really lasts once it's open, and how I store them so they're ready in December. One of them you can test in thirty seconds with a splash of vinegar. One goes stale faster than almost anything else in your pantry. And the one at number one is probably in your cupboard right now, and it may already be dead. We'll get there.

I'm Catherine, and I learned most of this the hard way, with a tray of flat cookies on a Christmas Eve. So here's what actually works.

First, why stock up now. Baking season is when stores put flour, granulated sugar and chocolate chips on sale, and this year that matters. The USDA expects sugar and sweets to cost around six to seven percent more this year. That includes the granulated sugar in almost every cookie and cake you make. And wheat at the farm is forecast to jump about seventeen percent. Chocolate has been one of the fastest-rising prices in the store this fall. One financial analyst quoted in the news this month put chocolate up around eleven to twelve percent, even though wholesale cocoa has fallen. So buying in November makes sense. The trick is buying only what you'll use, and storing it so it lasts.

For storage times, I'm using the food storage guide from North Dakota State University. It's one of the clearest references there is, and every number I give you comes from it unless I say otherwise.

Number twelve. Vanilla extract.

Vanilla is one of the most expensive bottles in the baking aisle, so it's worth storing right. Unopened, the NDSU guide gives pure vanilla about two years in the pantry. Once it's open, it keeps about twelve months.

What ruins it isn't time so much as air. The flavor in vanilla comes from volatile oils, and those slowly escape every time the cap sits loose. So close it tightly after every use, and keep it in a cool, dark cupboard, not on the shelf above the stove. If you open the bottle and the smell is faint, the flavor in your cookies will be faint too.

Next, number eleven. Ground cinnamon and the holiday spices.

Here's a surprise. Ground spices like cinnamon, ginger and nutmeg keep their quality about two to three years. Whole spices last three to four. But spice blends, like pumpkin pie spice, are listed at only about six months.

They won't hurt you after that. They just lose their flavor. So before you bake, open the jar and smell it. If you have to put your nose right in the jar to catch the scent, it's time for a new one. Store spices airtight and away from heat and sunlight. A drawer is better than a rack next to the range.

Number ten. Cornstarch.

This is the easy one. Unopened, cornstarch keeps eighteen to twenty-four months. Even after it's open, the NDSU guide gives it about eighteen months, as long as it stays tightly covered.

It thickens the gravy on Thanksgiving, which is on November twenty-sixth this year, and it thickens the fruit filling in a pie. One box covers the whole season. The only thing that hurts it is moisture, so keep the box closed and away from the sink.

Number nine. Sweetened condensed milk.

This is the base of fudge, pumpkin pie, magic bars and a lot of holiday candy. Unopened, a can keeps about twelve months in the pantry.

The mistake comes after you open it. Most recipes use one can, but some use half. Once it's open, sweetened condensed milk keeps only four to five days in the refrigerator. Don't leave it in the can. Scrape what's left into a small covered jar, and plan a second recipe for that week.

Number eight. Molasses.

If you bake gingerbread or molasses cookies, this is the jar that makes them taste like the holidays. Unopened, molasses keeps one to two years. Once it's open, it keeps about six months in the pantry.

The NDSU guide notes that refrigeration extends its storage. It pours slowly when it's cold, so I set the jar in a bowl of warm water for a few minutes before I measure. And wipe the rim before you put the lid back on. A sticky rim glues the lid shut and draws ants.

Number seven. Brown sugar.

Brown sugar keeps about eighteen to twenty-four months in an airtight container. But you already know its real problem. You open the bag for Christmas cookies, and it's a brick.

Brown sugar gets its softness from moisture, and that moisture escapes into the air. So once a bag is open, I move it into an airtight container or a heavy freezer bag and press out the air.

If it's already hard, don't throw it out. King Arthur Baking suggests putting a slice of soft sandwich bread on top of the sugar, sealing the container, and leaving it overnight. The sugar pulls moisture from the bread. In the morning, take the bread out and the sugar is soft again.

Number six. Shortening.

Pie crusts, biscuits and a lot of old family cookie recipes still call for shortening. Unopened, the NDSU guide gives it one to two years in a cool, dark place. It doesn't need refrigeration.

Once it's open, Iowa State University Extension says to expect about a year. The texture or color may change a little, and that's usually still fine. But fat can go rancid. If it smells sharp, sour or like old crayons, throw it away. Keep the lid tightly closed, and keep the can away from the warm stove.

Number five. Baking chocolate, cocoa powder and chocolate chips.

With chocolate prices this high, a sale in November is the time to buy. Unopened, baking chocolate keeps one to two years in the pantry. Once it's open, it keeps about a year. Unsweetened cocoa powder keeps even longer. The NDSU guide lists it as keeping indefinitely in the pantry, and it's worth stocking separately because it's the base of brownies, chocolate cakes and fudge frosting. Chocolate chips aren't listed separately in the guide, but I treat them the same way.

The key word is cool. Chocolate stored somewhere warm can develop a dusty, grayish-white coating. It's called bloom, and it happens when a little fat or sugar rises to the surface. It looks unappealing, but it's still safe to bake with, and it disappears once the chocolate melts. To prevent it, keep chocolate in a cool, dark cupboard, sealed tightly, and away from the oven.

Number four. Whole wheat flour.

Here's the one that goes stale faster than almost anything else in your pantry. White flour keeps six to twelve months unopened. Whole wheat flour gets only three to six.

That's because whole wheat flour still has the oil from the wheat germ, and oil goes rancid. Once it's open, the NDSU guide recommends the refrigerator, where it keeps six to eight months. I go one step further and keep mine in the freezer. White flour, by the guide, can be frozen for indefinite storage, and the cold helps whole grain flour keep its fresh flavor too.

There's a second reason to use the freezer. Kansas State University Extension says that holding packaged dry foods at zero degrees or lower for three days kills stored-food insects, and a large package may need seven. So a new bag of flour goes straight into my freezer for a week before it goes into the pantry. If I bake from it often, it moves into a jar with a screw-on lid, which the same publication calls the best container for keeping pests out.

Next up, number three. Yeast.

If you make dinner rolls or cinnamon rolls for the holidays, this is the one that decides whether they rise. Unopened dry yeast keeps about two years in the pantry. Once it's open, it keeps about four months in the refrigerator, or about six months in the freezer.

Here's how to know before you waste a whole batch of dough. Iowa State University Extension recommends a simple proof test. Stir one teaspoon of sugar into half a cup of warm water in a one-cup measure. Stir in one envelope of yeast and wait ten minutes. If it foams up to the half-cup mark, the yeast is working. If it just sits there, buy fresh yeast before you start.

Number two. Baking soda.

Unopened, baking soda keeps two to three years. Once the box is open, the NDSU guide gives it only three to six months.

This is the one you can test in thirty seconds with a splash of vinegar. Iowa State's test is one teaspoon of baking soda stirred into three tablespoons of vinegar. If it bubbles up right away, it's good. If it barely fizzes, replace it. And if you keep an open box in the refrigerator to absorb odors, don't bake with that box. Buy a separate one for the kitchen, and write the date you opened it on the side.

And number one. Baking powder.

This is the one that's probably in your cupboard right now, and it may already be dead.

Unopened, baking powder keeps six to eighteen months. Once it's open, it keeps only three to six months. That's shorter than almost everything else on this list. And it's the most common reason holiday baking turns out flat, dense or heavy. The biscuits don't rise. The cake sinks in the middle. And the can looks exactly the same as the day you bought it.

Baking powder works by reacting with liquid and heat. Moisture in the air slowly uses up that reaction, a little more every time you open the lid. So a can you opened last Christmas is very likely too weak for this one.

Here's the test. Iowa State University Extension says to stir one teaspoon of baking powder into a third of a cup of hot water. If it bubbles, it's still good. If it barely reacts, it's done its job, and a new can costs far less than a ruined batch of cookies. Keep the lid pressed down tight, keep the can dry, and use a dry spoon every time. Buy the smallest can you'll finish in a few months, and write the opening date on the lid.

So that's the list. Vanilla, spices, cornstarch, sweetened condensed milk, molasses, brown sugar, shortening, chocolate and cocoa powder, whole wheat flour, yeast, baking soda and baking powder. And of course the everyday staples like granulated sugar, powdered sugar for frostings, butter, eggs and canned pumpkin puree are worth checking too, though most of those turn over fast enough that freshness isn't usually the problem.

Here's my routine before the holidays. On the first weekend of November, I pull every baking staple out of the cupboard. I smell the spices and the vanilla. I test the baking powder, the baking soda and the yeast. Anything that fails goes on the shopping list, and I buy it when the holiday sales start. New flour spends a week in the freezer. Brown sugar goes into an airtight container. Every lid gets a date.

It takes about twenty minutes. And it means the cookies rise, the rolls puff up, and the pie tastes like the holidays instead of like the back of a cupboard.
