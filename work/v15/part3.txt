Here's how to know before you waste a whole batch of dough. Iowa State University Extension recommends a simple proof test. Stir one teaspoon of sugar into half a cup of warm water in a one-cup measure. Stir in one envelope of yeast and wait ten minutes. If it foams up to the half-cup mark, the yeast is working. If it just sits there, buy fresh yeast before you start.

Number two. Baking soda.

Unopened, baking soda keeps two to three years. Once the box is open, the NDSU guide gives it only three to six months.

This is the one you can test in thirty seconds with a splash of vinegar. Iowa State's test is one teaspoon of baking soda stirred into three tablespoons of vinegar. If it bubbles up right away, it's good. If it barely fizzes, replace it. And if you keep an open box in the refrigerator to absorb odors, don't bake with that box. Buy a separate one for the kitchen, and write the date you opened it on the side.

And number one. Baking powder.

This is the one that's probably in your cupboard right now, and it may already be dead.

Unopened, baking powder keeps six to eighteen months. Once it's open, it keeps only three to six months. That's shorter than almost everything else on this list. And it's the most common reason holiday baking turns out flat, dense or heavy. The biscuits don't rise. The cake sinks in the middle. And the can looks exactly the same as the day you bought it.

Baking powder works by reacting with liquid and heat. Moisture in the air slowly uses up that reaction, a little more every time you open the lid. So a can you opened last Christmas is very likely too weak for this one.

Here's the test. Iowa State University Extension says to stir one teaspoon of baking powder into a third of a cup of hot water. If it bubbles, it's still good. If it barely reacts, it's done its job, and a new can costs far less than a ruined batch of cookies. Keep the lid pressed down tight, keep the can dry, and use a dry spoon every time. Buy the smallest can you'll finish in a few months, and write the opening date on the lid.

So that's the list. Vanilla, spices, cornstarch, sweetened condensed milk, molasses, brown sugar, shortening, chocolate and cocoa powder, whole wheat flour, yeast, baking soda and baking powder. And of course the everyday staples like granulated sugar, powdered sugar for frostings, butter, eggs and canned pumpkin puree are worth checking too, though most of those turn over fast enough that freshness isn't usually the problem.

Here's my routine before the holidays. On the first weekend of November, I pull every baking staple out of the cupboard. I smell the spices and the vanilla. I test the baking powder, the baking soda and the yeast. Anything that fails goes on the shopping list, and I buy it when the holiday sales start. New flour spends a week in the freezer. Brown sugar goes into an airtight container. Every lid gets a date.

It takes about twenty minutes. And it means the cookies rise, the rolls puff up, and the pie tastes like the holidays instead of like the back of a cupboard.
