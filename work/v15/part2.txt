Number eight. Molasses.

If you bake gingerbread or molasses cookies, this is the jar that makes them taste like the holidays. Unopened, molasses keeps one to two years. Once it's open, it keeps about six months in the pantry.

The NDSU guide notes that refrigeration extends its storage. It pours slowly when it's cold, so I set the jar in a bowl of warm water for a few minutes before I measure. And wipe the rim before you put the lid back on. A sticky rim glues the lid shut and draws ants.

Number seven. Brown sugar.

Brown sugar keeps about eighteen to twenty-four months in an airtight container. But you already know its real problem. You open the bag for Christmas cookies, and it's a brick.

Brown sugar gets its softness from moisture, and that moisture escapes into the air. So once a bag is open, I move it into an airtight container or a heavy freezer bag and press out the air.

If it's already hard, don't throw it out. King Arthur Baking suggests putting a slice of soft sandwich bread on top of the sugar, sealing the container, and leaving it overnight. The sugar pulls moisture from the bread. In the morning, take the bread out and the sugar is soft again.

Number six. Shortening.

Pie crusts, biscuits and a lot of old family cookie recipes still call for shortening. Unopened, the NDSU guide gives it one to two years in a cool, dark place. It doesn't need refrigeration.

Once it's open, Iowa State University Extension says to expect about a year. The texture or color may change a little, and that's usually still fine. But fat can go rancid. If it smells sharp, sour or like old crayons, throw it away. Keep the lid tightly closed, and keep the can away from the warm stove.

Number five. Baking chocolate, cocoa powder and chocolate chips.

With chocolate prices this high, a sale in November is the time to buy. Unopened, baking chocolate keeps one to two years in the pantry. Once it's open, it keeps about a year. Unsweetened cocoa powder keeps even longer. The NDSU guide lists it as keeping indefinitely in the pantry, and it's worth stocking separately because it's the base of brownies, chocolate cakes and fudge frosting. Chocolate chips aren't listed separately in the guide, but I treat them the same way.

The key word is cool. Chocolate stored somewhere warm can develop a dusty, grayish-white coating. It's called bloom, and it happens when a little fat or sugar rises to the surface. It looks unappealing, but it's still safe to bake with, and it disappears once the chocolate melts. To prevent it, keep chocolate in a cool, dark cupboard, sealed tightly, and away from the oven.

Number four. Whole wheat flour.

Here's the one that goes stale faster than almost anything else in your pantry. White flour keeps six to twelve months unopened. Whole wheat flour gets only three to six.

That's because whole wheat flour still has the oil from the wheat germ, and oil goes rancid. Once it's open, the NDSU guide recommends the refrigerator, where it keeps six to eight months. I go one step further and keep mine in the freezer. White flour, by the guide, can be frozen for indefinite storage, and the cold helps whole grain flour keep its fresh flavor too.

There's a second reason to use the freezer. Kansas State University Extension says that holding packaged dry foods at zero degrees or lower for three days kills stored-food insects, and a large package may need seven. So a new bag of flour goes straight into my freezer for a week before it goes into the pantry. If I bake from it often, it moves into a jar with a screw-on lid, which the same publication calls the best container for keeping pests out.

Next up, number three. Yeast.

If you make dinner rolls or cinnamon rolls for the holidays, this is the one that decides whether they rise. Unopened dry yeast keeps about two years in the pantry. Once it's open, it keeps about four months in the refrigerator, or about six months in the freezer.
