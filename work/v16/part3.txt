But here's the catch, and it's why they're not higher. Rice and pasta need water and heat. In a winter outage, you may have neither to spare. So they belong in your supply, but they shouldn't be the only thing in it. Pair them with foods you can eat straight from the can.

Number three. Canned meat.

Ready.gov lists ready-to-eat canned meats first, along with a can opener. Canned chicken, tuna, salmon and beef are cooked protein you can eat cold if you have to. Like every can on this list, rotate them into your regular meals, so the oldest ones get eaten first and nothing sits for years.

And please don't skip the can opener. A pantry full of cans with no manual opener is a pantry you can't use. Keep one with your supplies, not in the kitchen drawer where it gets borrowed.

Number two. Vitamins.

This is the one most people never think to store. Ready.gov lists vitamins, and FEMA's guide recommends including vitamin, mineral and protein supplements in your stockpile. When you're living on canned food and crackers for a week, a daily vitamin helps fill the gaps. If anyone in your family takes medicine every day, keep a few extra days of it with your supplies too.

And number one. Peanut butter.

This is the one that needs no stove, no refrigerator and no can opener. It's on both FEMA lists. It's protein and calories in a jar, children will eat it, and it goes on crackers, on bread or straight off a spoon. FEMA's guide puts it in the use-within-one-year group. Keep the jar sealed until you need it, and keep one unopened jar with your emergency supply at all times.

So that's FEMA's list. Now, how much is enough?

Ready.gov's minimum is a few days. FEMA's own guide suggests two weeks. And the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, which has encouraged home food storage for generations, asks families to build a three-month supply of the foods they normally eat, and then add longer-term staples like wheat, white rice and beans, which it says can last thirty years or more when they're packaged and stored properly.

What I like most about the Church's guidance is the warning that comes with it. It says not to go to extremes, and that it isn't prudent to go into debt to build your food storage all at once. Start with one extra week. Buy a few extra items each grocery trip. Then grow from there.

Two last things that matter in winter.

Water. FEMA says one gallon per person per day, and more for children, nursing mothers and anyone who's sick. Its guide also says something I've never forgotten. If supplies run low, never ration water. Drink what you need today, and look for more tomorrow.

And the order you eat in. When the power goes off, FEMA's guide says to use the perishable food in the refrigerator first. Then the freezer. Only after that, start on the pantry. The USDA says an unopened refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours, and a full freezer holds its temperature for about forty-eight, or twenty-four if it's half full. Your pantry is the last line, not the first.

If you take one thing from this video, make it this. Look at your shelves tonight and ask one question about every item. Could my family eat this without power? If the answer is no for most of it, start this week with a jar of peanut butter, a few cans of meat and soup, a box of crackers and a gallon of water per person.
