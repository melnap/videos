Crackers are on the Ready.gov list, with one specific note. It says to choose salt-free crackers and to avoid foods that will make you thirsty. In an emergency, water is precious, and salty snacks make you drink more of it.

Crackers are also one of the shortest keepers on the list. FEMA's guide says to use dry, crisp crackers within about six months. Its storage tip is simple. Wrap them in plastic bags and keep them in tight containers. A stale cracker is still safe. It's just discouraging when it's what's for dinner.

Number ten. Dried fruit.

Dried fruit is on the Ready.gov list because it's sweet, light and needs nothing. Raisins on oatmeal or a handful of dried apricots feel like a treat on a hard day.

FEMA's guide puts dried fruit in the use-within-six-months group. It also says to empty opened packages of dried fruit, nuts and sugar into screw-top jars or airtight cans to keep pests out. That's a habit worth starting today.

Number nine. Nuts.

Nuts are calories, protein and fat in a small space, which is exactly what your body needs when the house is cold. Ready.gov lists them, and FEMA's guide puts canned nuts in the use-within-one-year group. Nuts go rancid over time because of their oil, so buy what your family will eat in a year and rotate them.

Number eight. Canned juice.

Ready.gov lists canned juices, and FEMA's guide groups canned fruit juices with the foods to use within a year. Juice counts toward the liquid your body needs, it gives children something they'll actually drink, and it adds vitamin C to a menu of mostly canned food.

Number seven. Shelf-stable milk.

Ready.gov lists non-perishable pasteurized milk, the kind in sealed cartons that needs no refrigeration until it's opened. FEMA's guide adds powdered milk. In a regular box, it says to use it within six months. In nitrogen-packed cans, it lists powdered milk with the foods that keep indefinitely. If you have children, this is one to have on the shelf. And once a carton is open, it needs the cold like any other milk.

Number six. Canned soup.

FEMA's guide lists canned condensed meat and vegetable soups in the use-within-one-year group. In winter, a can of soup is the closest thing to a hot meal you can make on a camp stove or even eat lukewarm. Ready.gov also suggests canned foods with a high liquid content, and soup is the best example. It feeds you and it hydrates you at the same time.

Number five. Canned fruits and vegetables.

These are on both lists. FEMA's guide says to use them within about a year. Canned vegetables round out rice and beans, and canned fruit is often the only fresh-tasting thing on day four.

Here's FEMA's rule for storing every can on this list. Keep them in a dry, cool, dark spot. Put new cans at the back and older ones in front. And date your supplies with a marker, so you always know what to use first. One more rule of my own. If a can is swollen, leaking or badly dented, don't use it.

Number four. White rice and dry pasta.

FEMA's guide lists both with the foods that can be stored indefinitely in proper containers and conditions. In plain words, that means airtight, dry and cool, not the thin bag from the store. They're cheap, filling and they stretch everything else on this list.
