Your Costco cart probably has the wrong things in it for winter.

That's not your fault. Costco is built to make the biggest package feel like the best deal, and sometimes it is. But a three-liter bottle of olive oil that goes stale by spring isn't a bargain. Neither is a giant box of fresh spinach that turns to slime on day four, or a five-dozen flat of eggs bought the same year egg prices are falling.

The real savings at Costco come from the foods that hold their value all winter. They're cheaper per pound than the grocery store, and they keep for months when you store them right.

Number ten. Sugar.

Sugar and sweets are among the categories the USDA expects to rise fastest this year, by around six to seven percent. With the baking season coming, that adds up. A big bag of granulated sugar keeps eighteen to twenty-four months in the pantry, according to the North Dakota State University food storage guide.

The mistake people make is leaving it in the paper bag on a shelf near the stove or the dishwasher. Steam and humidity get in, and you end up with a brick. Pour it into an airtight bin the day you bring it home, and it will still pour like new next fall.

Number nine. Rice.

Costco's twenty-five-pound bag of jasmine rice ran about twenty-one to twenty-three dollars at many warehouses this summer. That's under a dollar a pound. The national average for plain long-grain white rice was a dollar eleven a pound in August. So you're getting the nicer rice for less than the basic kind.

White rice keeps one to two years when it's tightly covered. But twenty-five pounds in a paper sack on the garage floor is an invitation for moisture and pantry bugs. I split it into smaller airtight containers. One stays in the kitchen, and the rest go on a cool, dry shelf. If one container ever has a problem, the others are safe.

Number eight. Flour.

This is the one that costs about a third of a dollar a pound. Costco's twenty-five-pound bag of all-purpose flour was around eight and a half dollars at many warehouses this summer.

Remember that wheat forecast? A rise at the farm takes time to reach the shelf, which is exactly why a bag bought now makes sense. White flour keeps six to eight months in the pantry once it's open, and about a year in the refrigerator. And the NDSU guide says it can be frozen for indefinite storage. So I keep one container in the kitchen and put the rest in freezer bags. When I need more, I let a bag come to room temperature before I open it, so no moisture gets in.

Number seven. Canned chicken.

Kirkland's six-pack of twelve-and-a-half-ounce cans was about ten dollars at many warehouses in late September, and up to fourteen in others. That's seventy-five ounces of cooked chicken. On a cold night it becomes soup, chicken salad or a casserole in fifteen minutes.

Unopened canned meat keeps for years in a cool cupboard. The NDSU guide lists up to five. Here's the detail most people miss. Once you open a can, don't put it in the refrigerator in the can. Move the chicken into a glass or plastic container, cover it, and use it within three to four days. Food left in an opened can can pick up a metallic taste.

Number six. Frozen vegetables.

Remember the USDA forecast? Fresh vegetables are expected to rise close to seven percent this year. In winter, fresh produce also travels farther, and it spoils faster than we'd like. Costco's big bags of frozen broccoli, green beans and mixed vegetables let you skip all of that.

Frozen vegetables are picked and frozen at their peak. They keep several months in the freezer. The NDSU guide puts it at up to eight. The one rule is to keep them frozen solid. If a bag has thawed and refrozen into one big lump, the quality has already suffered. And freezer burn becomes a real problem when air gets to the surface. Press the air out of the bag before you seal it every single time, and you'll keep freezer burn to a minimum.

Number five. Frozen berries.

A five-pound bag of Kirkland frozen blueberries was about twelve to sixteen dollars at many warehouses this year. That's roughly two and a half to three dollars a pound, for berries that are already washed and ready.

Here's why that matters. Fresh berries last two to three days in the refrigerator. Frozen berries keep eight to twelve months in the freezer. In January, a handful in your oatmeal costs you pennies, and nothing goes to waste.

The mistake is letting the bag sit open on the counter while you make breakfast. Take out what you need, press the air out, seal it, and put it right back. Pressing out that air also helps keep freezer burn away. Don't refreeze berries that have fully thawed.

Number four. Nuts.

Walnuts, pecans and almonds come in big bags at Costco, right when holiday baking starts. But here's the surprise. Nuts are full of oil, and that oil goes rancid. The NDSU guide gives most packaged nuts only about three months unopened in the pantry. Once they're open, they keep four to six months in the refrigerator. For longer storage, freeze them.

So that big bag on your pantry shelf is quietly running against a clock. Mine goes straight into the freezer in a few smaller bags. Frozen nuts don't need thawing for baking. And if a nut ever tastes bitter or smells like old paint, it has turned. Throw that bag away.

Number three. Chicken breasts.

Costco's ten-pound bag of thin-sliced chicken breasts, individually wrapped, was about thirty dollars at many warehouses this year. That's around three dollars a pound. The national average for boneless chicken breast was a little over four dollars a pound earlier this year.

Because each piece is wrapped on its own, you only take out what you need, which makes this one of the best bulk buys for meal prep. Batch a few pieces on Sunday and your lunches for the week are done. Frozen chicken pieces keep about nine months in the freezer. The mistake is thawing them on the counter. Move tomorrow's dinner from the freezer to the refrigerator tonight. It's safer, and the chicken stays juicier.

Number two. Coffee.

Coffee has been one of the most expensive items in the store. The national average for ground coffee was nine dollars and thirty cents a pound in August. Costco's three-pound bag of Kirkland Colombian whole bean coffee was about twenty-five dollars regularly, and around twenty-one on sale earlier this month. That's roughly seven to eight dollars a pound.

But three pounds is a lot of coffee. Once a bag is open, coffee keeps its flavor for only about two weeks in the pantry and about a month in the refrigerator. In the freezer, it keeps six to twelve months. So I divide a new bag into jars that each last about a week. One jar stays out, and the rest go in the freezer. Every cup tastes like the bag was just opened.

And number one. Butter.

This is the one you probably keep in the refrigerator, and it belongs in the freezer.

This summer, Costco's four-pound box of Kirkland butter was under ten dollars at many warehouses. That's around two and a half dollars a pound. The national average in August was about four dollars a pound. For a family that bakes through the holidays, that difference is real money.

But butter only keeps one to two months in the refrigerator. Four pounds is more than most homes can finish in that time. In the freezer, butter keeps six to nine months. So I keep one pound in the refrigerator and the rest in the freezer, wrapped tightly so it doesn't pick up freezer smells. When one pound runs out, the next one moves down. That's it. That one habit turns the best deal in the store into a deal you actually get to keep.

Now, the five Costco foods I never stock up on.

The first is the giant bottle of olive oil. Costco's three-liter bottle of extra virgin olive oil is a good price per ounce. But once it's open, the NDSU guide gives cooking oils about three to five months in the pantry. If you use a tablespoon a day, three liters lasts over six months. The last part of that bottle will be stale or rancid before you reach it. Buy the one-liter size, keep it in a cool, dark cupboard, and buy again when it's empty.

The second is the big containers of ground spices and spice blends. Ground spices lose their flavor after two to three years, and blends after about six months. A container that size takes most families years to finish. You end up seasoning food with dust that only smells like cinnamon. Buy whole spices if you want to buy in bulk, or buy the small jars.

The third is the giant packs of fresh produce. That huge clamshell of fresh berries lasts two to three days. A big box of salad greens lasts three to five, and about two once it's opened. Unless you're feeding a crowd this week, buy the frozen version, which you already saw on my list.

The fourth is the pre-portioned five-pack of ground beef. I wanted this one to be a deal. But at around thirty-four dollars for five pounds, it works out to about six eighty a pound. The national average for ground beef in September was seven dollars and six cents. That's only about a quarter less per pound. And ground beef only keeps three to four months in the freezer, so you can't stock up for long. Compare the unit price with your grocery store's sales before you put it in your cart.

And the fifth is the five-dozen flat of eggs. This is the one in almost every cart. But egg prices have been falling all year as flocks recover from the avian flu outbreaks that drove the egg price crisis over the past couple of years. Farm prices for eggs this summer were far below a year earlier. Eggs in the shell keep three to five weeks in the refrigerator. Buying five dozen ahead in a falling market only gives you a refrigerator full of eggs racing the calendar. Buy what you'll use this month.

So here's the whole idea in one sentence. Buy big only when the food keeps longer than it takes you to use it. Rice, flour, sugar and canned chicken keep for many months on the shelf. Butter, coffee, nuts, chicken and berries keep for months in the freezer. The giant oil bottle, the spice tub, the fresh produce and the eggs don't keep long enough to pay off.

If you take one thing home from your next Costco trip, make it a roll of freezer bags and a marker. Write the date on everything, and always pull the oldest item forward when you put something new away. That is the pantry organization principle called first in, first out, and it is the single habit that prevents waste. A little attention to pantry organization is what turns a big cart into a full pantry that lasts until spring.
