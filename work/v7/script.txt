If you're going to stock up before November, stock up on the right things. Grocery prices are moving this fall, just not all in the same direction.

A year ago, a pound of ground beef cost about six dollars and thirty cents on average. This August it was close to six ninety-two. At the same time, a dozen eggs has been getting cheaper, and cooking oil is expected to follow. Fill your cart with the wrong things now, and you pay this year's top price for food that would have cost less in December, or that goes stale before you open it.

That isn't careless shopping. The price tag never tells you which way a price is heading.

So here are fifteen groceries worth buying before November and the Thanksgiving rush, with why each one is worth buying now, how to store it in the pantry or the freezer, and how long it really keeps. One of them you'll only find fresh in the fall. One is among the cheapest proteins in the whole store. And at the end, five things I'd wait on, because their prices are going the other way.

Here is the bigger picture first. In August, groceries overall cost about two percent more than a year earlier, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. That's mild food inflation on average, but the average hides a wide spread. In its September outlook, the USDA forecast that beef and veal prices will finish twenty twenty-six about nine percent higher than last year, and sugar and sweets more than six percent higher. Eggs are forecast to end the year almost thirty percent cheaper. And Thanksgiving falls on November twenty-sixth this year, so the holiday shopping rush is only weeks away.

Good food storage decides whether stocking up actually saves you anything. Pantry food keeps best somewhere cool, dry and dark, not in the cabinet over the stove. Frozen food kept at zero degrees Fahrenheit stays safe indefinitely, according to the USDA. The freezer times you'll hear are for best quality, not safety.

Number one is white flour. Holiday baking starts soon, and flour is the base of almost everything on that list. An unopened bag keeps six to twelve months in the pantry, according to North Dakota State University Extension, and in the freezer it keeps indefinitely. There's a second reason to freeze it for a few days when it comes home. University of Minnesota Extension says that zero degrees for at least four days kills pantry insects and their eggs. A family that bakes through the holidays usually goes through more flour than it expects, so one or two bags beyond your normal use is a sensible start. Whole wheat flour is a different story, and it's on the wait list at the end.

Number two is sugar, white and brown. The sugar and sweets category is one of the faster risers this year. Sugar itself barely ages. North Dakota State gives it eighteen to twenty-four months for best quality, and if it stays dry it keeps far longer than that. It just needs an airtight container instead of the paper bag. If brown sugar turns hard, it isn't spoiled, it has simply dried out.

Number three is evaporated milk. It's the quiet ingredient in most pumpkin pies, and it's one of the cans everyone needs in the same few weeks before Thanksgiving. Unopened, it keeps about twelve months. Once it's open, it goes into the refrigerator and gets used within four or five days. It also makes soups and macaroni and cheese creamy without fresh milk, which is handy on a winter week when the carton runs out.

Number four is canned pumpkin and canned green beans, the Thanksgiving vegetables. The USDA expects processed fruit and vegetable prices to rise faster than their long-term average this year. Low-acid cans like these keep their best quality for two to five years, as long as the can stays sound. So buying in October isn't a gamble. Whatever you don't use for the holiday is still good for winter soups. One thing to check on the label: you want plain pumpkin, not pumpkin pie filling, which already has sugar and spices in it.

Number five is canned tomatoes. They're in the same rising category, and they anchor half the winter dinners in most kitchens: chili, soup, pasta sauce, a pot roast. One difference matters here. Tomatoes are high in acid, and the USDA gives high-acid cans twelve to eighteen months for best quality, not years. So buy what you'll use by next summer, and keep the oldest cans in front. Diced, crushed and sauce all keep about the same, so buy the forms you actually cook with.

Number six is broth, in cans or cartons. Stuffing, gravy, soup and a dozen weeknight dinners all start with it, and you'll want it on hand all through November. Canned broth keeps two to five years unopened, according to North Dakota State. Once a carton is opened, it lasts only a few days in the refrigerator, so smaller cartons often waste less than the big ones. If you watch salt in your house, the low-sodium versions keep just as long.

Number seven is plain unsweetened cocoa. Chocolate is behind much of the rise in sweets prices, and cocoa powder is the part of it that keeps almost forever. Iowa State University Extension says an unopened container keeps indefinitely, and about three years after opening. If you bake brownies, cocoa cake or hot cocoa from scratch, this is the cheapest way to have chocolate on hand all winter. Keep the lid on tight, because cocoa pulls in moisture and turns lumpy.

Number eight is baking chocolate and chocolate chips. Dark and semisweet chocolate keeps at least two years, according to Iowa State. Store it around sixty-five to seventy degrees, and not in the refrigerator, where it picks up moisture and smells. If you ever find gray or white streaks on old chocolate, that's called bloom. It's fat or sugar rising to the surface, and it doesn't make the chocolate unsafe. Milk chocolate keeps only about a year, and white chocolate about six months, so semisweet is the one to stock. Keep the bags away from strong-smelling food like onions and coffee, because chocolate absorbs odors.

Number nine is pecans and walnuts. Nuts are a holiday baking staple, and they're also the item on this list that goes stale fastest. Their oils turn rancid. Shelled nuts in ordinary bags keep only about three months in the pantry, according to North Dakota State, while a sealed vacuum can lasts about a year. The answer is the freezer. Buy them while the baking aisle is stocked, and put them straight into a freezer bag, pressing out as much air as you can.

Number ten is fresh cranberries, and this is the one you'll only find fresh in the fall, around the holidays. Fresh cranberries keep about two months in the refrigerator, and about twelve months in the freezer. Freeze them as they are, and rinse them when you use them. A few bags in October give you cranberry sauce, muffins and quick bread all the way into next summer, and frozen berries go straight into the batter.

Number eleven is dried beans. When beef climbs, beans are one of the cheapest proteins in the store, and they don't need a freezer at all. North Dakota State gives dried beans about twelve months for best quality. Older beans are still safe, but they take longer to cook, so use the oldest bag first. A pot of beans stretches a small amount of ground beef into chili for the whole family. Cook a big batch on a quiet afternoon, freeze it in two-cup portions, and you have ready beans for busy weeknights without paying for cans.

Number twelve is canned tuna and salmon. Fish and seafood prices were about six and a half percent higher in August than a year earlier. Canned fish is a low-acid can, so it keeps its best quality for two to five years when the can is in good shape. It's a protein that needs no freezer space and no power, which makes it useful in winter for more than one reason. When you stock up, look at the seams. Skip any can that is bulging, leaking, or deeply dented along the rim.

Number thirteen is ground coffee. Retail coffee prices were about six percent higher this August than last, according to the USDA. A pound of ground coffee averaged about nine dollars and thirty cents. Here's the honest part, though. That price peaked in April at nearly nine seventy-two, and it has drifted down a little since. So buy coffee on a good sale, not in a rush. An unopened can keeps up to two years in the pantry. Once it's open, it starts losing flavor within about two weeks. The freezer holds it six to twelve months. If you buy several cans, keep the extras sealed and open one at a time.

Number fourteen is a beef roast. A chuck roast or rump roast is where beef pays off most in the freezer. The USDA reports that the national beef cow herd stood at twenty-eight and a half million head in July, down one percent from a year earlier, and production is expected to stay below last year through the end of twenty twenty-six. That's why beef is forecast to rise the most of any major food. A roast keeps four to twelve months in the freezer. Wrap it tightly, or put the store package inside a freezer bag, so air can't reach the meat. One roast can easily become three dinners: the roast itself, sandwiches the next day, and a pot of beef and vegetable soup at the end of the week.

Number fifteen is ground beef. This is the biggest price jump on the list, about sixty cents a pound in a single year. Ground beef keeps three to four months in the freezer for best quality, a lot shorter than a roast. So buy what your family will use by February, not more. And the biggest mistake is freezing a five-pound tray as one solid brick. Split it into one-pound flat bags on the day you bring it home. Flat bags freeze faster, stack neatly, and thaw in the refrigerator overnight. Write the date on every bag, and the oldest one always comes out first.

Before you buy any of this, take ten minutes with your own kitchen. Look at what's already in the pantry and the freezer, and write down what your family actually goes through in a normal week. Stocking up only saves money on food that gets eaten. A case of something nobody likes is not a bargain, whatever it cost. And check your freezer space before the meat goes in. A freezer packed with a few extra roasts and bags of ground beef is a good thing. A freezer so full that the door doesn't close properly is not.

Now the five I'd wait on.

The first is eggs. They're forecast to end the year almost thirty percent cheaper than last year, and they don't keep long anyway.

The second is cooking oil. The USDA expects fats and oils prices to fall this year, and an unopened bottle keeps only about six months, according to North Dakota State. Buying a big supply now means paying more for something that goes rancid sooner.

The third is butter and other dairy. Dairy prices were slightly lower in August than a year ago. Butter does freeze well, about six to nine months, but there's no reason to rush. Wait for the holiday sales and freeze it then.

The fourth is baking powder. It costs little and seems like an easy thing to stock, but once it's open it loses its lift in three to six months. Buy one small can, not three big ones.

And the fifth is whole wheat flour. Its natural oils make it go stale much faster than white flour, in about three to six months on the shelf. If you bake with it, buy small amounts or keep it in the freezer.

The idea behind all of this is simple. Buy ahead on the things that keep and are getting more expensive. Let the things that are getting cheaper wait. Then put every new purchase behind the older ones, so the oldest gets used first. Done that way, the shopping you do in the next two weeks carries your kitchen well into the new year, at this fall's prices instead of next spring's.
