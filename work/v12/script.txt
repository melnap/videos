If the power went out tonight and stayed off for three days, your family could be hungry by day two, standing in front of a full pantry. Because when the power goes out, the only food that really helps is food that needs no stove, no refrigerator and no running water, and most pantries have almost none of it.

Picture it. A winter storm comes through on a Tuesday night, with ice on every power line. The lights flicker and go out. By morning the house is cold, the refrigerator is quiet, and the roads are too slick to go anywhere. You open the pantry, and it's full of food. Dry beans. Rice. Pasta. A bag of flour. And none of it does you any good, because every one of those needs a stove and water to cook.

That happens to a lot of careful families. Their pantry is built for normal weeks, not for the days the power is off. What's missing is a small emergency food supply that works without electricity.

So here are ten pantry foods to keep for when the power goes out. Every one of them can be eaten straight from the package. No cooking, no refrigerator, and nothing left over that has to be kept cold. For each one, I'll tell you how long it keeps and the one mistake that makes it useless in an outage. And one of them breaks a rule I gave you in an earlier video, on purpose. I'll explain why when we get there.

But first, what to do in the first hours, because the order you eat things in matters.

According to the USDA, a closed refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours. A full freezer keeps food frozen for about forty-eight hours, and a half-full one about twenty-four. So in an outage, you eat in this order. First the perishable food from the refrigerator, within those first hours, while it's still cold. Then food from the freezer, if you have a safe way to cook it. And the pantry comes last. That way, the shelf-stable food on this list waits for the day you really need it.

Keep the refrigerator and freezer doors shut as much as you can. If the outage looks like it will last longer than four hours, the USDA suggests moving milk, meat and leftovers into a cooler with ice, with a thermometer inside. And once the power has been off more than four hours, the USDA says perishable food like meat, milk, cut fruit and leftovers that has been above forty degrees for two hours or more should be thrown away. Don't taste it to test it. When in doubt, throw it out.

Some foods in the refrigerator door are more forgiving. Hard cheeses like cheddar and parmesan, butter, ketchup, mustard, pickles, jelly and peanut butter usually hold up fine through a longer outage, because they're dry, salty or acidic. Those can join your outage meals. A slice of cheddar on a cracker on day two is a real gift.

Now, the list.

Number one is peanut butter. It's the backbone of an outage pantry. Protein, fat and real fullness, in a jar that needs nothing. Unopened, it keeps six to twenty-four months in the pantry, according to North Dakota State University Extension. Once opened, about two to three months. Here's the mistake. Natural peanut butter, the kind where the oil separates on top, has to go in the refrigerator after you open it. For the outage shelf, keep the regular kind, which stays fine in the pantry. Spread it on crackers, eat it with a spoon, or put it on an apple while the fruit still lasts.

Number two is crackers. They're the bread of a power outage. Unopened, crackers keep about eight months. Once opened, about one month, and much less if the air is damp. That's the mistake to avoid. Buy the boxes with sleeves inside, and only open one sleeve at a time. The rest stay sealed and crisp. Crackers go with the peanut butter, with the tuna from number four, or alone when nobody's very hungry.

Number three is shelf-stable milk. This is milk that's been heated to a very high temperature and sealed in a box, so it doesn't need refrigeration until you open it. Check the date on the carton. It usually gives you months on the pantry shelf. The mistake is buying the big quart cartons. Once open, it needs the refrigerator just like regular milk, and you won't have one. So buy the small single-serving boxes, the kind made for lunch boxes. Each one is opened and finished at once. And that's the rule I mentioned. In my video on canned foods for tight months, I said single-serve packages aren't worth the money. In a normal week, that's true. In a power outage, there's no refrigerator for leftovers, so small packages are exactly what you want. For this one shelf, the rule flips.

Number four is tuna or chicken in pouches. Cans work, too, but pouches have two advantages in an outage. You don't need a can opener, and there's usually no liquid to drain, which matters when water is short. Check the date when you buy them, and keep them in a cool cupboard. Once a pouch is open, eat it at that meal. Without a refrigerator, nothing gets saved for later. Mix it with a single-serve mayonnaise packet, or just eat it on crackers.

Number five is ready-to-eat cereal. Not oatmeal that needs hot water, but the cold kind, the kind you'd pour into a bowl. With a box of shelf-stable milk from number three, it's a familiar breakfast for kids on a cold morning, and that matters more than it sounds. Unopened, cereal keeps six to twelve months, according to North Dakota State. Once opened, two to three months. Fold the inner bag down tight. Plain cereal and granola also work as a snack straight from the box.

Number six is dried fruit. Raisins, apricots, prunes and dried cranberries. In an outage, there's usually no fresh fruit after the first few days, and dried fruit fills that gap. It's sweet and filling, and kids will eat it. Unopened, it keeps about six months in the pantry. Once opened, about a month, or longer if it's sealed tight. Mix it with the nuts from number seven, and you've made trail mix.

Number seven is nuts. They're one of the most calorie-dense foods you can keep, which means a handful goes a long way. But here's the mistake almost everyone makes. Nuts go stale and rancid faster than people think, because of the oil in them. In a regular bag, North Dakota State gives them about three months unopened. In a vacuum-sealed can, about a year. So for the outage shelf, buy nuts in sealed cans or jars, not soft bags, and put the open bags in the freezer for everyday use.

Number eight is fruit cups and applesauce cups. The single-serve kind, for the same reason as the milk. Each one is opened, eaten and gone. A full can of fruit opened in an outage has to be finished in one sitting, because opened canned fruit only keeps a few days even in a refrigerator. Fruit cups also give you some liquid, which helps when water is limited. Choose the ones packed in juice, not heavy syrup.

Number nine is granola bars and protein bars. They're the easiest food on the list. No plate, no spoon, nothing to open but a wrapper. Keep a box in the pantry and a few in your car, because winter storms don't always catch you at home. Check the dates. They don't last forever. Rotate them into lunch boxes, and replace them as you go.

And number ten is a little comfort food. Cookies, chocolate, hard candy, or a box of the cereal the kids love. This isn't a joke. Emergency planners recommend keeping some comfort food in an emergency supply, because a long, cold, dark day is hard on everyone, and a familiar treat makes it easier. Unopened chocolate keeps one to two years. Packaged cookies about two months, so rotate those. Keep it small, and save it for the second or third day, when everyone needs it most.

Before we get to supplies, here's what doesn't belong on this shelf, even though it's in most pantries.

Instant noodles and cups of soup need boiling water. Rice, pasta and dry beans need a stove and a lot of water. Powdered milk only works if you have clean water to spare. Boxed meals, baking mixes and anything frozen are no help at all. Keep all of those for normal weeks. They're good food, just not outage food.

And think about the people in your house who eat differently. A baby needs formula or baby food that doesn't need mixing with heated water, and enough of it. Someone on a special diet needs their own few days of safe food. And don't forget the dog or the cat. Pet food belongs in the same box.

Now a few things that aren't food, but make every item on this list work.

Water comes first. FEMA's Ready campaign recommends at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and basic cleaning, and enough for several days. Keep it in sealed bottles or jugs. And if you filled the empty space in your freezer with water bottles, like I showed you in the freezer video, those keep the freezer cold and become drinking water as they melt.

Second, a manual can opener. If any of your outage food is in cans, an electric opener won't help you. Keep a hand opener in the same box as the food. With one, the canned beans, chili and fruit from my canned-food video work here too, as long as you're happy to eat them cold.

Third, go easy on salty foods. Salty chips and crackers make you thirsty, and in an outage, water is the one thing you can't make more of. Keep salty snacks to a minimum on this shelf.

And fourth, never cook indoors with anything that burns fuel. A grill, a camp stove, or a generator inside the house or garage can fill the air with carbon monoxide, which you can't see or smell. The CDC warns that it can kill. If you want hot food, use only what's made for indoor use. Otherwise, this list is here exactly so you don't need to cook.

So how much should you keep? Start with three days for everyone in the house. Most winter outages are shorter than that, and three days is what emergency planners recommend as a starting point. Put it all in one box, your emergency food kit, or on one shelf, separate from the everyday pantry, so nobody eats it on a normal Tuesday. Write the earliest date on the outside. And every six months, when the clocks change, eat what's close to its date and buy fresh.

Here's what three days from this shelf looks like. Breakfast is cereal with a box of milk and a fruit cup. Lunch is tuna on crackers with dried fruit. Dinner is peanut butter on crackers, a fruit cup and a handful of nuts, with a cookie at the end. It isn't fancy. But it's filling, it's familiar, and it doesn't need a single thing the storm took away.

And keep that box where you can find it in the dark. Put a flashlight and extra batteries right on top, along with the can opener, a roll of paper towels and a few plastic spoons. When the lights go out at night, you shouldn't be digging through the back of a closet with your phone as a flashlight.

If an outage ever runs longer than three days, this shelf buys you the time to make a plan. Check on your neighbors, especially older ones living alone. Find out where your town opens a warming center. And if you have a grill, it's fine to cook the thawing food from your freezer outside, well away from the house, never in the garage. The pantry shelf isn't meant to last forever. It's meant to carry you calmly through the first hard days.

Your first step is simple. This week, find one shelf or one box. Put in a jar of peanut butter, a box of crackers and a few small boxes of milk. Add the rest one or two items at a time when you shop. Before the first big storm, you'll have a pantry that works with the lights off.
