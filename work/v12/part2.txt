Number three is shelf-stable milk. This is milk that's been heated to a very high temperature and sealed in a box, so it doesn't need refrigeration until you open it. Check the date on the carton. It usually gives you months on the pantry shelf. The mistake is buying the big quart cartons. Once open, it needs the refrigerator just like regular milk, and you won't have one. So buy the small single-serving boxes, the kind made for lunch boxes. Each one is opened and finished at once. And that's the rule I mentioned. In my video on canned foods for tight months, I said single-serve packages aren't worth the money. In a normal week, that's true. In a power outage, there's no refrigerator for leftovers, so small packages are exactly what you want. For this one shelf, the rule flips.

Number four is tuna or chicken in pouches. Cans work, too, but pouches have two advantages in an outage. You don't need a can opener, and there's usually no liquid to drain, which matters when water is short. Check the date when you buy them, and keep them in a cool cupboard. Once a pouch is open, eat it at that meal. Without a refrigerator, nothing gets saved for later. Mix it with a single-serve mayonnaise packet, or just eat it on crackers.

Number five is ready-to-eat cereal. Not oatmeal that needs hot water, but the cold kind, the kind you'd pour into a bowl. With a box of shelf-stable milk from number three, it's a familiar breakfast for kids on a cold morning, and that matters more than it sounds. Unopened, cereal keeps six to twelve months, according to North Dakota State. Once opened, two to three months. Fold the inner bag down tight. Plain cereal and granola also work as a snack straight from the box.

Number six is dried fruit. Raisins, apricots, prunes and dried cranberries. In an outage, there's usually no fresh fruit after the first few days, and dried fruit fills that gap. It's sweet and filling, and kids will eat it. Unopened, it keeps about six months in the pantry. Once opened, about a month, or longer if it's sealed tight. Mix it with the nuts from number seven, and you've made trail mix.

Number seven is nuts. They're one of the most calorie-dense foods you can keep, which means a handful goes a long way. But here's the mistake almost everyone makes. Nuts go stale and rancid faster than people think, because of the oil in them. In a regular bag, North Dakota State gives them about three months unopened. In a vacuum-sealed can, about a year. So for the outage shelf, buy nuts in sealed cans or jars, not soft bags, and put the open bags in the freezer for everyday use.

Number eight is fruit cups and applesauce cups. The single-serve kind, for the same reason as the milk. Each one is opened, eaten and gone. A full can of fruit opened in an outage has to be finished in one sitting, because opened canned fruit only keeps a few days even in a refrigerator. Fruit cups also give you some liquid, which helps when water is limited. Choose the ones packed in juice, not heavy syrup.

Number nine is granola bars and protein bars. They're the easiest food on the list. No plate, no spoon, nothing to open but a wrapper. Keep a box in the pantry and a few in your car, because winter storms don't always catch you at home. Check the dates. They don't last forever. Rotate them into lunch boxes, and replace them as you go.

And number ten is a little comfort food. Cookies, chocolate, hard candy, or a box of the cereal the kids love. This isn't a joke. Emergency planners recommend keeping some comfort food in an emergency supply, because a long, cold, dark day is hard on everyone, and a familiar treat makes it easier. Unopened chocolate keeps one to two years. Packaged cookies about two months, so rotate those. Keep it small, and save it for the second or third day, when everyone needs it most.
