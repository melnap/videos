Before we get to supplies, here's what doesn't belong on this shelf, even though it's in most pantries.

Instant noodles and cups of soup need boiling water. Rice, pasta and dry beans need a stove and a lot of water. Powdered milk only works if you have clean water to spare. Boxed meals, baking mixes and anything frozen are no help at all. Keep all of those for normal weeks. They're good food, just not outage food.

And think about the people in your house who eat differently. A baby needs formula or baby food that doesn't need mixing with heated water, and enough of it. Someone on a special diet needs their own few days of safe food. And don't forget the dog or the cat. Pet food belongs in the same box.

Now a few things that aren't food, but make every item on this list work.

Water comes first. FEMA's Ready campaign recommends at least one gallon per person per day, for drinking and basic cleaning, and enough for several days. Keep it in sealed bottles or jugs. And if you filled the empty space in your freezer with water bottles, like I showed you in the freezer video, those keep the freezer cold and become drinking water as they melt.

Second, a manual can opener. If any of your outage food is in cans, an electric opener won't help you. Keep a hand opener in the same box as the food. With one, the canned beans, chili and fruit from my canned-food video work here too, as long as you're happy to eat them cold.

Third, go easy on salty foods. Salty chips and crackers make you thirsty, and in an outage, water is the one thing you can't make more of. Keep salty snacks to a minimum on this shelf.

And fourth, never cook indoors with anything that burns fuel. A grill, a camp stove, or a generator inside the house or garage can fill the air with carbon monoxide, which you can't see or smell. The CDC warns that it can kill. If you want hot food, use only what's made for indoor use. Otherwise, this list is here exactly so you don't need to cook.

So how much should you keep? Start with three days for everyone in the house. Most winter outages are shorter than that, and three days is what emergency planners recommend as a starting point. Put it all in one box, your emergency food kit, or on one shelf, separate from the everyday pantry, so nobody eats it on a normal Tuesday. Write the earliest date on the outside. And every six months, when the clocks change, eat what's close to its date and buy fresh.

Here's what three days from this shelf looks like. Breakfast is cereal with a box of milk and a fruit cup. Lunch is tuna on crackers with dried fruit. Dinner is peanut butter on crackers, a fruit cup and a handful of nuts, with a cookie at the end. It isn't fancy. But it's filling, it's familiar, and it doesn't need a single thing the storm took away.

And keep that box where you can find it in the dark. Put a flashlight and extra batteries right on top, along with the can opener, a roll of paper towels and a few plastic spoons. When the lights go out at night, you shouldn't be digging through the back of a closet with your phone as a flashlight.

If an outage ever runs longer than three days, this shelf buys you the time to make a plan. Check on your neighbors, especially older ones living alone. Find out where your town opens a warming center. And if you have a grill, it's fine to cook the thawing food from your freezer outside, well away from the house, never in the garage. The pantry shelf isn't meant to last forever. It's meant to carry you calmly through the first hard days.

Your first step is simple. This week, find one shelf or one box. Put in a jar of peanut butter, a box of crackers and a few small boxes of milk. Add the rest one or two items at a time when you shop. Before the first big storm, you'll have a pantry that works with the lights off.
