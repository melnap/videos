If the power went out tonight and stayed off for three days, your family could be hungry by day two, standing in front of a full pantry. Because when the power goes out, the only food that really helps is food that needs no stove, no refrigerator and no running water, and most pantries have almost none of it.

Picture it. A winter storm comes through on a Tuesday night, with ice on every power line. The lights flicker and go out. By morning the house is cold, the refrigerator is quiet, and the roads are too slick to go anywhere. You open the pantry, and it's full of food. Dry beans. Rice. Pasta. A bag of flour. And none of it does you any good, because every one of those needs a stove and water to cook.

That happens to a lot of careful families. Their pantry is built for normal weeks, not for the days the power is off. What's missing is a small emergency food supply that works without electricity.

So here are ten pantry foods to keep for when the power goes out. Every one of them can be eaten straight from the package. No cooking, no refrigerator, and nothing left over that has to be kept cold. For each one, I'll tell you how long it keeps and the one mistake that makes it useless in an outage. And one of them breaks a rule I gave you in an earlier video, on purpose. I'll explain why when we get there.

But first, what to do in the first hours, because the order you eat things in matters.

According to the USDA, a closed refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours. A full freezer keeps food frozen for about forty-eight hours, and a half-full one about twenty-four. So in an outage, you eat in this order. First the perishable food from the refrigerator, within those first hours, while it's still cold. Then food from the freezer, if you have a safe way to cook it. And the pantry comes last. That way, the shelf-stable food on this list waits for the day you really need it.

Keep the refrigerator and freezer doors shut as much as you can. If the outage looks like it will last longer than four hours, the USDA suggests moving milk, meat and leftovers into a cooler with ice, with a thermometer inside. And once the power has been off more than four hours, the USDA says perishable food like meat, milk, cut fruit and leftovers that has been above forty degrees for two hours or more should be thrown away. Don't taste it to test it. When in doubt, throw it out.

Some foods in the refrigerator door are more forgiving. Hard cheeses like cheddar and parmesan, butter, ketchup, mustard, pickles, jelly and peanut butter usually hold up fine through a longer outage, because they're dry, salty or acidic. Those can join your outage meals. A slice of cheddar on a cracker on day two is a real gift.

Now, the list.

Number one is peanut butter. It's the backbone of an outage pantry. Protein, fat and real fullness, in a jar that needs nothing. Unopened, it keeps six to twenty-four months in the pantry, according to North Dakota State University Extension. Once opened, about two to three months. Here's the mistake. Natural peanut butter, the kind where the oil separates on top, has to go in the refrigerator after you open it. For the outage shelf, keep the regular kind, which stays fine in the pantry. Spread it on crackers, eat it with a spoon, or put it on an apple while the fruit still lasts.

Number two is crackers. They're the bread of a power outage. Unopened, crackers keep about eight months. Once opened, about one month, and much less if the air is damp. That's the mistake to avoid. Buy the boxes with sleeves inside, and only open one sleeve at a time. The rest stay sealed and crisp. Crackers go with the peanut butter, with the tuna from number four, or alone when nobody's very hungry.
