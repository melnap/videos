Walmart has eleven pantry staples, every single one under five dollars, and together they can stock your whole winter pantry for about thirty-three dollars.

The catch is that most people buy them, store them wrong, and throw half away by March. The rice gets bugs. The peanut butter goes stale. The tomatoes sit open in the can and taste like metal. That isn't bad luck. Nobody ever told you how long each one really lasts, or what it needs.

So today I'm walking you through the eleven pantry staples I stock every fall at Walmart, all under five dollars, and exactly how I store each one so it lasts all winter. Every one of them is Great Value, Walmart's own brand. One of them makes a serving of broth for about eight cents. One of them earns its place under my sink as much as in my pantry. And the one at number one is the cheapest real protein in the whole store. We'll get there.

I checked every price on Walmart's website this week. Prices change by store and by week, so treat them as a guide. But when I added up one of each of these eleven, the total came to about thirty-three dollars.

Here's why this matters right now. Grocery prices overall rose a little under two percent over the past year, according to the latest government report. But the USDA expects beef to cost more than nine percent more this year. When meat gets expensive, the plain staples on the bottom shelf are what keep dinner affordable. Knowing which cheap staples to lean on is real food security. These are those staples.

Number eleven. White vinegar.

A gallon of Great Value distilled white vinegar was three ninety-four this week. That's about three cents an ounce.

In my kitchen, vinegar does a lot of quiet work. A splash in a cup of milk makes a stand-in for buttermilk when you're baking. A little in a pot of beans or a quick pickle brightens the whole thing. And yes, it cleans the coffee maker and the windows too.

In winter, I also use it for quick refrigerator pickles. Cabbage, carrots and onions are some of the cheapest vegetables in the store this time of year. Slice them thin, pour over a warm mix of vinegar, water, salt and a little sugar, and keep the jar in the refrigerator. It turns a two-dollar head of cabbage into a side dish for the week.

The North Dakota State University food storage guide gives white vinegar about two years unopened and a year once it's open. And it notes that distilled white vinegar keeps longer than cider vinegar. If it ever looks a little cloudy, that doesn't affect the quality. Keep it tightly capped, and that one gallon carries you through the whole winter and then some.

Next up at number ten. Popcorn kernels.

A thirty-two-ounce bag of Great Value yellow popping corn was two forty-seven. That's under eight cents an ounce. A quarter cup of kernels makes a big bowl, so you're looking at well under a quarter per bowl.

On a long winter evening, that's a snack the whole family shares for pennies. It's a whole grain, too. And it keeps. The NDSU guide gives popcorn kernels about two years stored in an airtight container. Microwave popcorn bags, by comparison, keep only six to twelve months, and they cost several times more per bowl.

The mistake is leaving the bag open in a warm cupboard. Pour the kernels into a jar with a tight lid. They stay ready for every movie night until spring.

Next up at number nine. Chicken bouillon.

This is the one that makes a serving of broth for about eight cents. A three-point-seven-five-ounce jar of Great Value chicken bouillon powder was two dollars and eight cents, and the label lists about twenty-five servings.

A carton of ready-made broth costs far more per cup. With a jar of bouillon on the shelf, you can turn rice, beans and a few vegetables into soup any night of the week.

Bouillon keeps about a year, according to the NDSU guide, as long as it stays dry and covered. Here's the mistake that ruins it. Steam from the pot gets into the jar when you spoon it out over the stove, and the powder turns into a hard lump. Always use a dry spoon, and measure it away from the steam.
