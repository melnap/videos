Your freezer is probably costing you money right now, and you think it's saving you.

Open it tonight and look. Two pounds of ground beef with no date on them. Chicken with white, leathery edges. A foil package nobody can name. Every one of those went in to save money, and a lot of it will end up in the trash by spring.

Here's the hard truth. A freezer doesn't keep food good. It only slows the clock. Set up wrong, it's a cold closet where groceries go to be forgotten. Set up right, before winter, it carries your family through January without a single panic trip to the store.

That isn't carelessness. Most of us were taught to put frozen food away, not how to run a freezer as real food storage.

So here's how to set up your freezer before winter, in twenty tricks that save food. They're grouped the way a winter freezer actually works: setting it up, what to stock this season, what never goes in, and how to thaw and protect it when the weather turns. One trick costs a few dollars and tells you whether your freezer is really doing its job. One holiday food keeps a full year frozen, and almost nobody buys an extra bag. And the last one uses a single coin to answer the question every family asks after a storm. We'll get there.

Part one. Set the freezer up.

Number one. Check the temperature. Your freezer should sit at zero degrees Fahrenheit or colder. The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service says food kept constantly at zero stays safe indefinitely. The times you see on freezer charts are for quality, not safety. But the dial inside most freezers just says one to five. It doesn't tell you a number. A simple appliance thermometer costs a few dollars. Put it in the middle of the freezer, check it the next morning, and turn the dial if you need to. Everything else on this list depends on that number.

Number two. Fill the empty space before winter. A full freezer holds its temperature much longer than a half-empty one. According to the USDA, a full freezer keeps food frozen for about forty-eight hours without power, and a half-full one for about twenty-four. That's true for an upright freezer and a chest freezer alike. That's a whole extra day during an ice storm. If you don't have enough food to fill it yet, fill the gaps with plastic bottles of water, leaving an inch at the top so they don't split. They take up the space now, and if the power goes out, they become ice for your refrigerator.

Number three. Air is the enemy, not cold. Freezer burn is those dry, grayish-white patches on meat. It happens when air reaches the surface of the food and dries it out. The USDA says freezer-burned food is still safe to eat. It just tastes dry and tough, so cut those spots away. The fix is less air. Use bags labeled for the freezer, not thinner storage bags, and press out every bit of air before you seal them. If you freeze a lot of meat, a vacuum sealer is worth having, because it takes out nearly all the air. And here's the part most people miss. The thin plastic wrap on a supermarket meat tray isn't made for long freezing. If that package is going to sit for more than a month or two, the USDA recommends wrapping it again, right over the store package, with heavy foil, freezer paper or a freezer bag.

Number four. Freeze in the size you cook. If your family eats one pound of ground beef for a meal, the five-pound family pack never goes into the freezer as one block. Split it into five one-pound bags the day you bring it home. Press each bag flat, so it freezes thin and thaws faster. Chicken thighs go in bags of four or six, whatever feeds your table. You never have to thaw more than you need, and nothing thawed sits in the refrigerator waiting for a plan.

Number five. Keep a list on the door. Labels tell you what's in a bag once you're holding it. A list tells you what's in the freezer before you open it. Tape a sheet of paper to the outside of the door. Write down what goes in and the date. When something comes out, cross it off. It takes ten seconds. In January you'll stand at the door and plan a week of dinners without letting out the cold, and nothing will hide at the bottom until spring.
