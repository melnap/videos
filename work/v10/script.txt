Your freezer is probably costing you money right now, and you think it's saving you.

Open it tonight and look. Two pounds of ground beef with no date on them. Chicken with white, leathery edges. A foil package nobody can name. Every one of those went in to save money, and a lot of it will end up in the trash by spring.

Here's the hard truth. A freezer doesn't keep food good. It only slows the clock. Set up wrong, it's a cold closet where groceries go to be forgotten. Set up right, before winter, it carries your family through January without a single panic trip to the store.

That isn't carelessness. Most of us were taught to put frozen food away, not how to run a freezer as real food storage.

So here's how to set up your freezer before winter, in twenty tricks that save food. They're grouped the way a winter freezer actually works: setting it up, what to stock this season, what never goes in, and how to thaw and protect it when the weather turns. One trick costs a few dollars and tells you whether your freezer is really doing its job. One holiday food keeps a full year frozen, and almost nobody buys an extra bag. And the last one uses a single coin to answer the question every family asks after a storm. We'll get there.

Part one. Set the freezer up.

Number one. Check the temperature. Your freezer should sit at zero degrees Fahrenheit or colder. The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service says food kept constantly at zero stays safe indefinitely. The times you see on freezer charts are for quality, not safety. But the dial inside most freezers just says one to five. It doesn't tell you a number. A simple appliance thermometer costs a few dollars. Put it in the middle of the freezer, check it the next morning, and turn the dial if you need to. Everything else on this list depends on that number.

Number two. Fill the empty space before winter. A full freezer holds its temperature much longer than a half-empty one. According to the USDA, a full freezer keeps food frozen for about forty-eight hours without power, and a half-full one for about twenty-four. That's true for an upright freezer and a chest freezer alike. That's a whole extra day during an ice storm. If you don't have enough food to fill it yet, fill the gaps with plastic bottles of water, leaving an inch at the top so they don't split. They take up the space now, and if the power goes out, they become ice for your refrigerator.

Number three. Air is the enemy, not cold. Freezer burn is those dry, grayish-white patches on meat. It happens when air reaches the surface of the food and dries it out. The USDA says freezer-burned food is still safe to eat. It just tastes dry and tough, so cut those spots away. The fix is less air. Use bags labeled for the freezer, not thinner storage bags, and press out every bit of air before you seal them. If you freeze a lot of meat, a vacuum sealer is worth having, because it takes out nearly all the air. And here's the part most people miss. The thin plastic wrap on a supermarket meat tray isn't made for long freezing. If that package is going to sit for more than a month or two, the USDA recommends wrapping it again, right over the store package, with heavy foil, freezer paper or a freezer bag.

Number four. Freeze in the size you cook. If your family eats one pound of ground beef for a meal, the five-pound family pack never goes into the freezer as one block. Split it into five one-pound bags the day you bring it home. Press each bag flat, so it freezes thin and thaws faster. Chicken thighs go in bags of four or six, whatever feeds your table. You never have to thaw more than you need, and nothing thawed sits in the refrigerator waiting for a plan.

Number five. Keep a list on the door. Labels tell you what's in a bag once you're holding it. A list tells you what's in the freezer before you open it. Tape a sheet of paper to the outside of the door. Write down what goes in and the date. When something comes out, cross it off. It takes ten seconds. In January you'll stand at the door and plan a week of dinners without letting out the cold, and nothing will hide at the bottom until spring.

Part two. What to stock this winter.

Number six. Cranberries. This is the holiday food almost nobody buys extra of. Fresh cranberries are only in stores for a few months in the fall. In the refrigerator they keep about two months. In the freezer, they keep about a year, according to North Dakota State University Extension. You don't even need to wash them first. Put the bags straight in the freezer and wash the berries when you use them. And don't thaw them before cooking. Use them frozen in sauce, muffins or a pan of baked oatmeal, and they hold their shape better. Buy two or three extra bags in November, and you'll have cranberries in March when nobody else does.

Number seven. A second turkey. A whole uncooked turkey keeps about a year in the freezer for best quality. Around Thanksgiving, many stores put turkeys on sale at a low price per pound to bring shoppers through the door. A turkey bought on that sale and frozen is a Sunday dinner in February, plus soup and sandwiches for days after. Keep it in its original wrapper, and add a second layer if the wrapper tears.

Number eight. Leftover turkey, frozen the right way. After the holiday meal, plain cooked turkey pieces keep about four months in the freezer. But covered in broth or gravy, they keep about six months, and they come out moist instead of dry. The USDA says leftovers keep three to four days in the refrigerator, so get them into the freezer well before that. Pull the meat off the bones within a day or two, pack it into containers in two-cup portions, and pour broth over it until it's covered. That's the start of a pot pie, a soup, or a skillet of turkey and rice on a cold night.

Number nine. Holiday ham, in small pieces. Fully cooked ham keeps only one to two months in the freezer for best quality, so this one is about using it, not saving it for spring. Dice the leftovers into one-cup bags for scrambled eggs, beans, and potato soup. And freeze the bone. A ham bone simmered with a pound of dried beans is one of the cheapest, most filling dinners of the whole winter.

Number ten. Soup, in two-cup portions. Soups and stews keep two to three months in the freezer. When you make a pot, make extra and freeze it in containers that each hold two cups, enough for one person's lunch or a side for two. Leave about an inch of space at the top, because liquid expands as it freezes and will push a lid off. Freeze them flat in bags if your freezer is tight. On the evening everyone comes home cold and tired, you're ten minutes from a hot meal.

Number eleven. Baked cookies, made early. Here's one that saves the busiest month of the year. Baked cookies keep eight to twelve months in the freezer, according to North Dakota State. So bake in early November, when the days are calmer, and freeze them in containers with wax paper between the layers. When December fills up, the cookie trays for church, school and family are already done. Most cookies thaw on the counter in about fifteen minutes.

Number twelve. Know which meat to eat first. Not all meat lasts the same. According to the USDA cold storage chart, steaks keep six to twelve months for best quality, and roasts four to twelve. Ground beef keeps only three to four months. Chicken pieces keep about nine. So when you organize the freezer, the ground beef goes in front and gets cooked first. The steaks and roasts can wait in the back for a special dinner in March.

Number thirteen. The short-timers. Some foods lose quality fast even when frozen. Bacon keeps only about one month. Hot dogs, lunch meat and sausage keep about one to two months. The salt in them makes the fat turn stale faster, even at zero degrees. So don't buy ten packs of bacon on sale and expect them to taste good in April. Buy what you'll use in a month or two, and put those packages where you'll see them.

Part three. What never goes in.

Number fourteen. Eggs in the shell and mayonnaise. Eggs expand as they freeze and crack their shells, and the USDA chart says to avoid freezing them in the shell. If you need to save eggs, crack them out first. Mayonnaise separates when it thaws and turns oily, and so do the salads made with it, like potato salad and egg salad. Those leftovers stay in the refrigerator, three to five days, and that's all.

Number fifteen. Salad greens and other watery vegetables. Lettuce, cucumbers, celery for snacking, and raw tomatoes are mostly water. When that water freezes, it breaks the plant cells, and they thaw limp and soggy. Chopped celery or tomatoes are fine for a soup pot. For a crisp salad, they're ruined. Don't fill freezer space with them. And vegetables you freeze yourself, like green beans or broccoli, need a quick blanch in boiling water first, or they come out dull and tough.

Number sixteen. Unopened cans. A can in the freezer is a mistake people make when they're trying to save leftovers or cool a drink fast. The food expands as it freezes, which can bend the can and break the seal along the seams. If you want to freeze the rest of a can of beans, tomato sauce or broth, move it into a freezer container first.

Part four. Thaw it safely and protect it.

Number seventeen. Thaw tomorrow's dinner tonight. The safest way to thaw is in the refrigerator, and it takes planning. Move tomorrow's meat from the freezer to a plate on the bottom shelf of the refrigerator tonight. A big turkey needs much longer. The USDA says about twenty-four hours for every four to five pounds, which is four full days for a twenty-pound bird. If you forget, thaw it in its sealed bag in cold water and change the water every thirty minutes. Never leave meat on the counter to thaw. The outside warms up long before the middle does.

Number eighteen. The refreezing rule. People throw away a lot of good food over this. If meat thawed in the refrigerator and you changed your plans, you can put it back in the freezer without cooking it, according to the USDA. It may lose a little texture, but it's safe. Food you thawed in cold water or in the microwave is different. Cook it first, and then you can freeze the cooked dish.

Number nineteen. The coin in a cup. This is the one I promised you. Fill a small cup with water and freeze it solid. Then lay a coin on top of the ice, and keep the cup in your freezer all winter. If you're away during a storm, or the power goes out at night, look at that coin when you get back. If it's still on top, the freezer stayed frozen. If it sank to the middle or the bottom, everything in there thawed at some point and then froze again, even if it looks frozen now. It costs nothing, and it answers a question you can't answer any other way.

And number twenty. When the power goes out, keep the door shut. Every time the door opens, the cold air you need pours out. A full freezer gives you about forty-eight hours, and a half-full one about twenty-four, but only if the door stays closed. Once the power is back, check your thermometer from number one. The USDA says food that still has ice crystals, or that stayed at forty degrees or below, can be refrozen. And any food you're unsure about gets thrown away. Don't taste it to find out. If you want to know what to keep in the pantry for those days without power, I'm making a whole video on that.

Those are the twenty. Now let me put them together, because a winter freezer isn't twenty separate tricks. It's one system.

The thermometer and the full shelves keep it cold. Freezer bags, a second layer of wrap and portion-size packages keep food from drying out. The list on the door tells you what you have. The front holds what needs to be eaten soon, like the ground beef and the bacon, and the back holds the turkey, the steaks and the cranberries for later. And the coin tells you whether it all held through a storm. Put together, that means far less food waste, and far fewer emergency trips to the store.

Here's your first step, and it's small. Sometime this week, take fifteen minutes and empty your freezer onto the counter. Throw out anything you can't identify. Write everything else on a sheet of paper and tape it to the door. Put a thermometer in the middle, and freeze one cup of water for the coin. Do just that, and you'll walk into winter knowing exactly what you have.
