Let's start with why November.

Every year, from early November until Christmas, butter is one of the foods stores fight over. Thanksgiving is on November twenty-sixth this year, and holiday baking follows it right into December. Stores know that if they put butter, flour and sugar on sale, families come in for them and fill the rest of the cart while they're there. So butter often turns into what stores call a loss leader, priced low on purpose to get you through the door. Watch the weekly ads starting the first week of November, and you'll usually see it. The sales often run right through Black Friday week, and some stores bring butter back on sale for Christmas baking in December.

Here are the numbers behind what I told you at the start. At the end of September, butter was trading on the wholesale market for about a dollar thirty-two a pound, according to USDA Dairy Market News. That's the price stores and food companies pay in bulk. Wholesale butter has been low for more than a year now, because butter supplies have been plentiful. In August, the USDA expected the wholesale price to average about a dollar sixty-five a pound for all of twenty twenty-six.

At the store, the price is much higher than that, of course. The Bureau of Labor Statistics found that a pound of butter averaged about four dollars and two cents in August. That's about seventeen percent less than a year earlier. When wholesale prices are this low and the holidays are coming, stores have plenty of room to run sales. That's exactly the moment to buy, and freezing is what lets you buy enough to matter.

So how long does butter really keep? In the refrigerator, about one to two months. In the freezer, about six to nine months for best quality, according to the USDA's FoodKeeper guide and North Dakota State University Extension. And if your freezer stays at zero degrees or colder, it stays safe even longer than that. The six to nine months is about flavor, not safety.

That means butter bought in the middle of November is still at its best in May or even July. One good sale can carry you through the whole winter and well into spring.

Now, how much to buy. This is where people either save money or waste it, so let's do the math together.

Look at how much butter your family uses in a normal week. Not a holiday week. A normal one. Maybe it's one stick, maybe it's a whole pound. Then think about the weeks between now and next spring. From mid-November to the end of April is about twenty-four weeks. If you use one stick a week, that's twenty-four sticks, which is six pounds. Then add what you'll bake for the holidays. A batch of cookies often takes one or two sticks, and a pie crust takes one. Write that number down before you go to the store.

Here's why that number matters. If you buy twelve pounds because the sale looks good, and you only use six by spring, the last six pounds will sit in your freezer, slowly picking up off-flavors. The savings disappear. Buy what you'll use in six months, and every pound is a good deal.

And check the sale for limits. Many stores allow only a few pounds per customer on their best butter deal. If you shop two weeks in a row, or your spouse makes a separate trip, you can usually reach your number without paying full price for any of it.

Next, which butter to buy. For baking, I keep unsalted butter, because most recipes are written for it and you can control the salt yourself. For the table, salted butter. Both freeze well. European-style butter, with a little more butterfat, freezes just as well. It just costs more, so I save it for the recipes where you can really taste it. Sticks are easier to freeze and thaw than one big block, because you only take out what you need. And look at the price per pound, not the price per package. A store brand on sale and a name brand on sale are often close in price, and for most cooking you won't taste a difference.

Now the most important part. How to wrap butter for the freezer.

Butter absorbs smells. It's mostly fat, and fat picks up the odors around it. In the refrigerator, that's why butter next to an onion can start to taste like onion. In the freezer, it's the fish, the garlic bread and the frost itself. The thin paper wrapper on each stick is fine for the refrigerator. It isn't enough for six months in the freezer.

So here's how I do it. Leave each stick in its own wrapper. Then leave the sticks in the box, and put the whole box inside a heavy freezer bag. Press out as much air as you can, and seal it. If you have more than one box, put each in its own bag, so you only open one at a time. Write the date on the bag with a marker.

If you bought a big block, cut it into half-pound or quarter-pound pieces first, wrap each piece tightly in plastic wrap, and then bag them. A big block takes a long time to thaw, and you don't want to thaw two pounds to use two tablespoons.

And where it goes matters, too. Put the butter in the back of the freezer, not in the door. The door is the warmest part of the freezer, and it warms up every time you open it. That small up and down is what slowly dulls the flavor. The back stays steady.

Now let's talk about using it.

To thaw a stick, move it from the freezer to the refrigerator the day before. That's it. By the next morning, it's ready to spread or measure. If you need softened butter for baking, leave it on the counter for about an hour after that. Butter is safe at room temperature for a day or two, according to the USDA, but it can start to taste stale after several days, and faster in a warm kitchen. So keep only one stick in the butter dish at a time, and the rest in the refrigerator or the freezer.

And here's the trick I promised you. For biscuits, scones and pie crust, you don't want soft butter. You want it as cold as possible. Take a stick straight from the freezer and grate it on the large holes of a box grater, right into your flour. Toss it with a fork, and the little shreds of frozen butter stay separate. In the hot oven they melt and leave little pockets of steam, which is what makes biscuits tall and pie crust flaky. It's the easiest pie crust you'll ever make, and it only works with frozen butter.

A few more ways to make frozen butter easier to use.

Cut a stick into tablespoon slices while it's still cold, lay them on a plate, freeze them, and then put them in a bag. Now you can drop one tablespoon into a pan of rice or a pot of vegetables without thawing anything.

Make flavored butter while you have extra. Soften a stick and mix in honey and a little cinnamon for toast and cornbread. Or mix in garlic and dried herbs for bread and potatoes. Roll it into a log in plastic wrap and freeze it. Slice off a round whenever you need it. This makes a plain weeknight dinner feel like a holiday.

Then there's brown butter. Melt a stick in a pan and keep cooking it, stirring, until it smells nutty and turns golden, then pour it into an ice cube tray. Each cube is a deep, rich flavor for cookies, oatmeal or a pot of squash soup.

Now the mistakes that ruin frozen butter.

Mistake one is freezing it in the thin store wrapper only. It picks up odors and gets freezer burn on the ends. Always put it in a sealed bag.

Mistake two is thawing and refreezing it over and over. Each time it thaws and freezes again, it loses a little quality. Take out one stick at a time.

Mistake three is forgetting it. Butter in the back of a freezer with no date on it gets found next fall, tasting stale. Date the bag, and keep the oldest butter in front.

Mistake four is buying more than your family will use in six months. A sale isn't a savings if half of it ends up tasting like the freezer.

And how do you know if frozen butter is still good? Smell it and look at it. Good butter smells sweet and mild. If it smells sour, like old cheese or paint, it has gone rancid, and it won't make anything taste better. Throw it out. A little white, dry patch on one end is freezer burn. Cut that part off, and the rest is fine.
