Make flavored butter while you have extra. Soften a stick and mix in honey and a little cinnamon for toast and cornbread. Or mix in garlic and dried herbs for bread and potatoes. Roll it into a log in plastic wrap and freeze it. Slice off a round whenever you need it. This makes a plain weeknight dinner feel like a holiday.

Then there's brown butter. Melt a stick in a pan and keep cooking it, stirring, until it smells nutty and turns golden, then pour it into an ice cube tray. Each cube is a deep, rich flavor for cookies, oatmeal or a pot of squash soup.

Now picture what that freezer full of butter actually does for you over a winter.

Thanksgiving week alone can take a pound or more. A double pie crust takes about two sticks. Mashed potatoes for a crowd take one or two more. Then there's the stuffing, the rolls and a pan of something sweet. When that butter is already in your freezer, Thanksgiving week is one less trip to a crowded store, on the one week when everybody else is buying butter too.

Then December comes, with cookie trays, a birthday cake, a church potluck. Every one of those starts with butter, and you never have to think about it.

And then there are the long, cold weeks of January and February, when the roads are bad and you're cooking from what you have. Butter is what turns a pantry meal into a real one. It's the roux that thickens a pot of potato soup or turns turkey drippings into gravy. It's the butter in a skillet of grilled cheese next to a bowl of tomato soup. It goes into a pan of cornbread with a pot of chili, a stack of pancakes on a snowy Saturday morning, and a pot of rice that tastes like more than rice.

At around four dollars a pound, every stick costs about a dollar. Bought on a good sale, it costs less. That's a dollar or less to make a whole family's dinner taste better, and it's sitting in the back of your freezer, ready.

Now the mistakes that ruin frozen butter.

Mistake one is freezing it in the thin store wrapper only. It picks up odors and gets freezer burn on the ends. Always put it in a sealed bag.

Mistake two is thawing and refreezing it over and over. Each time it thaws and freezes again, it loses a little quality. Take out one stick at a time.

Mistake three is forgetting it. Butter in the back of a freezer with no date on it gets found next fall, tasting stale. Date the bag, and keep the oldest butter in front.

Mistake four is buying more than your family will use in six months. A sale isn't a savings if half of it ends up tasting like the freezer.

And how do you know if frozen butter is still good? Smell it and look at it. Good butter smells sweet and mild. If it smells sour, like old cheese or paint, it has gone rancid, and it won't make anything taste better. Throw it out. A little white, dry patch on one end is freezer burn. Cut that part off, and the rest is fine.

So here's the simple plan. This week, figure out how much butter your family uses, and write that number down. Starting in November, watch the ads for the butter sales before Thanksgiving. When you see a good price, buy enough to reach your number, bag it, date it, and put it in the back of the freezer. Then use it one stick at a time all winter.

That's why I buy butter in November. Not because it's a trick, but because it lets me buy something we use every week on my schedule, at the price I choose, and stop thinking about it until spring.
