Here's why that number matters. If you buy twelve pounds because the sale looks good, and you only use six by spring, the last six pounds will sit in your freezer, slowly picking up off-flavors. The savings disappear. Buy what you'll use in six months, and every pound is a good deal.

And check the sale for limits. Many stores allow only a few pounds per customer on their best butter deal. If you shop two weeks in a row, or your spouse makes a separate trip, you can usually reach your number without paying full price for any of it.

Next, which butter to buy. For baking, I keep unsalted butter, because most recipes are written for it and you can control the salt yourself. For the table, salted butter. Both freeze well. European-style butter, with a little more butterfat, freezes just as well. It just costs more, so I save it for the recipes where you can really taste it. Sticks are easier to freeze and thaw than one big block, because you only take out what you need. And look at the price per pound, not the price per package. A store brand on sale and a name brand on sale are often close in price, and for most cooking you won't taste a difference.

Now the most important part. How to wrap butter for the freezer.

Butter absorbs smells. It's mostly fat, and fat picks up the odors around it. In the refrigerator, that's why butter next to an onion can start to taste like onion. In the freezer, it's the fish, the garlic bread and the frost itself. The thin paper wrapper on each stick is fine for the refrigerator. It isn't enough for six months in the freezer.

So here's how I do it. Leave each stick in its own wrapper. Then leave the sticks in the box, and put the whole box inside a heavy freezer bag. Press out as much air as you can, and seal it. If you have more than one box, put each in its own bag, so you only open one at a time. Write the date on the bag with a marker.

If you bought a big block, cut it into half-pound or quarter-pound pieces first, wrap each piece tightly in plastic wrap, and then bag them. A big block takes a long time to thaw, and you don't want to thaw two pounds to use two tablespoons.

And where it goes matters, too. Put the butter in the back of the freezer, not in the door. The door is the warmest part of the freezer, and it warms up every time you open it. That small up and down is what slowly dulls the flavor. The back stays steady.

Now let's talk about using it.

To thaw a stick, move it from the freezer to the refrigerator the day before. That's it. By the next morning, it's ready to spread or measure. If you need softened butter for baking, leave it on the counter for about an hour after that. Butter is safe at room temperature for a day or two, according to the USDA, but it can start to taste stale after several days, and faster in a warm kitchen. So keep only one stick in the butter dish at a time, and the rest in the refrigerator or the freezer.

And here's the trick I promised you. For biscuits, scones and pie crust, you don't want soft butter. You want it as cold as possible. Take a stick straight from the freezer and grate it on the large holes of a box grater, right into your flour. Toss it with a fork, and the little shreds of frozen butter stay separate. In the hot oven they melt and leave little pockets of steam, which is what makes biscuits tall and pie crust flaky. It's the easiest pie crust you'll ever make, and it only works with frozen butter.

A few more ways to make frozen butter easier to use.

Cut a stick into tablespoon slices while it's still cold, lay them on a plate, freeze them, and then put them in a bag. Now you can drop one tablespoon into a pan of rice or a pot of vegetables without thawing anything.
