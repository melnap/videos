Pumpkin pie is different. The same guide gives it only four to five weeks. It suggests pouring cold filling into a cold, unbaked shell, freezing it, and baking it unthawed. And one warning. Custard and cream pies don't freeze well. The crust turns soggy.

Number four. Gravy.

Leftover gravy usually goes down the drain. It doesn't have to. The Missouri guide says gravies and sauces keep three to four months in the freezer in rigid containers. Thaw them in the refrigerator and stir while you reheat.

Milk-based gravies can separate. So if your gravy is made with broth, it'll freeze better. And freeze it in small containers, so you can thaw just enough for one dinner of hot turkey sandwiches in January.

Number three. Stuffing.

The Missouri guide gives stuffing about one month in the freezer. To reheat it, thaw it partway in the refrigerator, add a little water, and heat it until it reaches one hundred sixty-five degrees all the way through.

One month isn't long, so label it clearly and plan to eat it before Christmas.

Number two. Leftover turkey.

This is where most holiday waste happens. The USDA advises never leaving food at room temperature for more than two hours. So within two hours of dinner, take the meat off the bones, slice it, and put it in shallow containers. Shallow containers cool faster than one deep dish.

Then freeze what you won't eat in a few days. The Missouri guide gives roast poultry two to four months in the freezer, and the FoodSafety.gov chart says cooked poultry keeps two to six months. Freeze it in meal-sized portions with a little broth to keep it moist, and you've got soup, pot pie and enchiladas waiting for you in the dead of winter.

And number one. Eggs.

This is the one almost nobody freezes, and it can rescue your Christmas baking. Not in the shell. The FoodSafety.gov chart is clear that eggs should never be frozen in the shell. But out of the shell, they freeze beautifully.

The National Center for Home Food Preservation explains how. Mix the yolks and whites together gently, without whipping in air. Freeze them in an ice cube tray, three tablespoons to a section. Each cube equals one egg. The same tray works for leftover broth.

Separated eggs freeze too, and this is what saves your leftovers from holiday baking. Egg whites need nothing added. Two tablespoons equals one white. Yolks are the tricky part. On their own, they turn thick and gummy in the freezer. So the guide says to stir in a pinch of salt or a little sugar before freezing, depending on whether you'll use them in savory or sweet dishes. Label which one you added. The FoodSafety.gov chart gives raw whites and yolks about twelve months in the freezer.

I'm not telling you to stock up on eggs. This is for the half carton that's about to pass its date, or the six yolks left over after an angel food cake.

So that's the list. Herbs, cranberries, heavy cream, cream cheese, hard cheese, milk, buttermilk, bread and rolls, cookie dough, pie crust, fruit pies, gravy, stuffing, turkey and eggs.

Here's the habit that ties it together. Before you throw anything away this holiday season, ask one question. Could this go in the freezer instead? Then freeze it flat, in small portions, with the air pressed out and the name and date written on the bag.

Do that, and this year's leftovers become January's easy dinners instead of this week's trash.
