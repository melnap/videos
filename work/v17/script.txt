Your holiday leftovers don't belong in the trash. Most of them belong in your freezer.

But that's not where most of them end up.

It goes like this every year. The half carton of cream left over from the pie. Four egg whites in a cup on the counter. The second bag of cranberries you bought on sale. Two cups of gravy nobody finished. A week later it all goes in the trash, along with the money you paid for it, and the time it took to make.

That isn't carelessness. Most of us learned that the freezer is for meat, ice cream and frozen peas. Nobody told us about the rest.

So today I'm sharing fifteen holiday foods you can freeze now, how to do each one right, and how long each one keeps, so your holiday cooking costs less and wastes less. One of them goes into the freezer straight from the bag, no washing. One only freezes well if it has enough fat in it. And the one at number one is something almost nobody freezes, and it can rescue your Christmas baking. We'll get there.

I'm Catherine, and I learned these the slow way, one wasted carton at a time. So here's what actually works.

Before we start, one rule that applies to everything on this list. The government's FoodSafety.gov chart says food kept frozen at zero degrees stays safe indefinitely. The storage times I'll give you are for quality, meaning flavor and texture. So a few weeks past isn't a reason to panic. It's a reason to use it soon. And the biggest threat to quality for every item on this list is freezer burn, the dry, leathery damage that happens when air reaches the food. Press the air out of bags, wrap things tightly, and use rigid containers with lids that seal. That one habit protects everything.

For the details, I'm using guides from North Dakota State University Extension, the University of Missouri Extension, Penn State Extension and the National Center for Home Food Preservation.

Number fifteen. Fresh herbs.

Thanksgiving recipes call for a few sprigs of sage, thyme or rosemary, and the store sells you a whole package. The rest wilts in the refrigerator.

The NDSU guide says to wash the herbs, drain them, pat them dry, wrap a few sprigs in freezer wrap and seal them in a container. One honest note. Frozen herbs go limp when they thaw, so they won't work as a garnish. But in stuffing, soup or gravy, you won't know the difference.

Number fourteen. Cranberries.

This is the one that goes into the freezer straight from the bag. Fresh cranberries are in stores for only a few months a year, and they're often on sale around Thanksgiving.

Penn State Extension says you can put the bags of fresh cranberries directly into the freezer for up to twelve months and use them without thawing. Just rinse them under cold water before you cook. So buy a few extra bags now, and you'll have cranberry sauce in March and cranberry muffins in June.

Number thirteen. Heavy cream.

This is the one that only freezes well if it has enough fat. The NDSU guide says to freeze only heavy cream with forty percent butterfat or more. Lighter cream and half-and-half don't freeze well.

There's one catch. Frozen cream won't whip up to its full volume. So use it in soups, sauces and mashed potatoes. And here's a trick from the same guide. If you have whipped cream left over from pie, sweeten it, spoon it in little mounds onto a tray, freeze them, and then bag them. They're perfect on hot cocoa. That trick is called flash freezing, freezing pieces separately on a tray so they don't clump, and it works for a lot of things on this list.

Number twelve. Cream cheese.

People are told cream cheese doesn't freeze, and for spreading on a bagel, that's true. The texture changes. But the NDSU guide says cream cheese can be frozen for later use in cooking, in dips or as icing.

So if you bought the big pack for cheesecake and dip, freeze the extra blocks in their foil wrappers inside a freezer bag. Let them thaw in the refrigerator and use them in anything baked or mixed.

Number eleven. Hard cheese.

A holiday cheese board always leaves leftovers. The NDSU guide says hard and semi-hard cheese can be frozen in pieces of no more than half a pound, sealed tightly. After thawing, it will be a little crumbly and won't slice neatly, but the flavor stays good.

That makes frozen cheese perfect for anything cooked. Macaroni and cheese, casseroles, soups. A Washington State University Extension handout gives hard cheeses like cheddar and Swiss about six months in the freezer.

Number ten. Milk.

Yes, milk freezes. The NDSU guide says pasteurized milk can be frozen, including low-fat and nonfat. The key is room to expand. Pour off a little from the top of the jug before it goes in, or the jug can split.

When it thaws, the milk may look a little separated. Shake it or stir it and it comes back. Thaw it in the refrigerator, use it soon, and don't refreeze it. It's a good way to save the half gallon that would otherwise go bad while you're traveling for the holidays.

Number nine. Buttermilk.

Most recipes need one cup of buttermilk, and the carton holds four. The NDSU guide groups buttermilk with the cultured dairy foods that turn a little grainy and may separate when frozen. But they still work perfectly in cooking.

So freeze the rest in one-cup portions, labeled. Next time you make biscuits, pancakes or cornbread, you won't have to buy another carton.

Number eight. Bread and rolls.

The University of Missouri Extension guide says baked bread and rolls keep about three months in the freezer. To bring them back, thaw them, wrap them in foil and warm them in a three-hundred-degree oven for about fifteen minutes. Rolls take five to ten.

That means you can bake or buy your dinner rolls two weeks before Thanksgiving, freeze them, and have one less thing to do on the busiest day of the year.

Number seven. Cookie dough.

The same Missouri guide gives cookie dough about three months in the freezer. Roll it into logs or scoop it into balls on a tray, flash freeze them on the tray until solid, then bag them. Logs of refrigerator-cookie dough can be sliced and baked without thawing. Bulk dough should thaw in the refrigerator until you can drop it by the spoonful.

Make your Christmas cookie dough in November, on a quiet weekend, and bake it fresh in December.

Number six. Pie crust.

Pie crust is the step that makes holiday baking feel like work. The Missouri guide says unbaked pie crust keeps six to eight weeks in the freezer, and you can bake it without thawing. A baked crust keeps two to three months.

So make three or four crusts at once, freeze them flat or in the pans, and the hardest part of Thanksgiving pie is done before the week even starts.

Number five. Unbaked fruit pies.

This one surprises people most. The Missouri guide gives unbaked fruit pies about eight months in the freezer. You bake them straight from frozen, and you cut the vents in the top crust after freezing, not before.

Pumpkin pie is different. The same guide gives it only four to five weeks. It suggests pouring cold filling into a cold, unbaked shell, freezing it, and baking it unthawed. And one warning. Custard and cream pies don't freeze well. The crust turns soggy.

Number four. Gravy.

Leftover gravy usually goes down the drain. It doesn't have to. The Missouri guide says gravies and sauces keep three to four months in the freezer in rigid containers. Thaw them in the refrigerator and stir while you reheat.

Milk-based gravies can separate. So if your gravy is made with broth, it'll freeze better. And freeze it in small containers, so you can thaw just enough for one dinner of hot turkey sandwiches in January.

Number three. Stuffing.

The Missouri guide gives stuffing about one month in the freezer. To reheat it, thaw it partway in the refrigerator, add a little water, and heat it until it reaches one hundred sixty-five degrees all the way through.

One month isn't long, so label it clearly and plan to eat it before Christmas.

Number two. Leftover turkey.

This is where most holiday waste happens. The USDA advises never leaving food at room temperature for more than two hours. So within two hours of dinner, take the meat off the bones, slice it, and put it in shallow containers. Shallow containers cool faster than one deep dish.

Then freeze what you won't eat in a few days. The Missouri guide gives roast poultry two to four months in the freezer, and the FoodSafety.gov chart says cooked poultry keeps two to six months. Freeze it in meal-sized portions with a little broth to keep it moist, and you've got soup, pot pie and enchiladas waiting for you in the dead of winter.

And number one. Eggs.

This is the one almost nobody freezes, and it can rescue your Christmas baking. Not in the shell. The FoodSafety.gov chart is clear that eggs should never be frozen in the shell. But out of the shell, they freeze beautifully.

The National Center for Home Food Preservation explains how. Mix the yolks and whites together gently, without whipping in air. Freeze them in an ice cube tray, three tablespoons to a section. Each cube equals one egg. The same tray works for leftover broth.

Separated eggs freeze too, and this is what saves your leftovers from holiday baking. Egg whites need nothing added. Two tablespoons equals one white. Yolks are the tricky part. On their own, they turn thick and gummy in the freezer. So the guide says to stir in a pinch of salt or a little sugar before freezing, depending on whether you'll use them in savory or sweet dishes. Label which one you added. The FoodSafety.gov chart gives raw whites and yolks about twelve months in the freezer.

I'm not telling you to stock up on eggs. This is for the half carton that's about to pass its date, or the six yolks left over after an angel food cake.

So that's the list. Herbs, cranberries, heavy cream, cream cheese, hard cheese, milk, buttermilk, bread and rolls, cookie dough, pie crust, fruit pies, gravy, stuffing, turkey and eggs.

Here's the habit that ties it together. Before you throw anything away this holiday season, ask one question. Could this go in the freezer instead? Then freeze it flat, in small portions, with the air pressed out and the name and date written on the bag.

Do that, and this year's leftovers become January's easy dinners instead of this week's trash.
