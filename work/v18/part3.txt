And number one. Canned food, still in the can.

This is the one that isn't about taste. It's about safety.

Cans and sealed jars get put in the freezer more often than you'd think. Leftover soup left in its can. A case of canned goods in a garage that drops below freezing. A jar pushed to the back of a freezer to chill quickly and forgotten. When the food inside freezes, it expands, and it can push the seams of the can open.

The USDA is very direct about this. If a frozen can has rusted or burst seams, throw it out immediately. And it says, in capital letters, do not taste it.

What to do instead. Never freeze food in its can. If you have leftovers from a can, move them into a freezer container, leaving some room at the top for expansion. And if you store canned goods in a garage or shed, bring them inside before the first hard freeze of winter.

Before I sum up, a few quick ones that didn't make the ten but still catch people every year.

Gelatin salads. Both the Washington State and Missouri guides say plain gelatin weeps after freezing. Make it the day before instead.

Fried foods. The Missouri guide says fried meat and poultry lose their crispness in the freezer. If you freeze fried chicken anyway, reheat it in the oven rather than the microwave.

Bacon, ham and other cured meats. They can be frozen, but not for long. The USDA gives bacon and sausage only one to two months in the freezer, and the Missouri guide says cured meats lose their color and turn rancid faster than fresh meat. Freeze them in small packs and use them first.

And half-and-half. North Dakota State says light cream and half-and-half don't freeze well. Only heavy cream with forty percent fat or more holds up. So buy the small carton of half-and-half for your coffee, and freeze heavy cream for cooking.

And one for seasonings. Washington State's guide says garlic, green pepper and cloves tend to turn strong and bitter in the freezer. So season a dish lightly before you freeze it, and add more when you reheat.

So that's the list. Lettuce, mayonnaise salads, eggs in the shell, hard-boiled eggs, sour cream, cream sauces, custard pies, boiled potatoes, pasta in sauce, and food left in the can.

One thing that won't rescue these foods is a vacuum sealer. The problem here is ice crystals and creams that separate, not air. A vacuum sealer is useful for meat, hard cheese and baked goods, where freezer burn is the main enemy. But it won't turn sour cream or boiled potatoes into foods that freeze well.

Two last things that save more food than any list.

First, refreezing. People throw away good food because they think thawed food can never go back in the freezer. The USDA says that food thawed in the refrigerator is safe to refreeze without cooking, though you may lose some quality. What you should never refreeze is food that was thawed on the counter. The USDA says the only safe ways to thaw are in the refrigerator, in cold water or in the microwave.

Second, freezer burn. Those gray, leathery patches don't make food unsafe. The USDA says freezer burn just means air reached the surface and dried it out. Cut those spots away and use the rest.

If you take one thing from this video, let it be this. Before something goes into the freezer, ask yourself two questions. Is it mostly water, like lettuce or a boiled potato? Or is it creamy, like mayonnaise or a cream sauce? If the answer to either one is yes, freeze the parts, not the dish.
