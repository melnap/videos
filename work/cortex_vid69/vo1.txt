A coworker makes one small comment in a meeting. Your face gets hot, your answer comes out sharper than you meant, and on the drive home you replay it and wonder why that person always gets to you. Here's the thing: they're pulling a lever you didn't know you had. And the most popular advice about anger, let it out, actually makes that lever stronger. In the next few minutes you'll see what psychology has tested instead: seven steps that take the lever out of their hands, without swallowing your feelings or pretending to be endlessly patient.

In two thousand two, the psychologist Brad Bushman published a study that quietly destroyed the most popular piece of advice about anger. People are told to let it out, to vent, to hit a pillow, to get it off their chest. Psychologists call this the catharsis hypothesis, the idea that releasing anger drains it. Bushman tested that advice directly, and it failed. Once you understand why it failed, the lever starts to lose its power over you.

Here is what he did. Students wrote a short essay and handed it in for feedback. The feedback that came back was brutal and personal, written on purpose to make them angry. Then the students were split into groups. One group hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who had insulted them. Another group hit the same punching bag while thinking about getting fit. A third group simply sat quietly for a couple of minutes and did nothing at all.

Afterwards, every student got the chance to blast the person who insulted them with loud noise through headphones. The group that vented while thinking about that person was the angriest and the most aggressive of all. The group that did nothing was the calmest. Venting did not drain the anger. It trained it.

That one result changes the whole game. Anger is not pressure in a pipe that has to escape somewhere. It is a response that grows stronger every time you rehearse it. And anyone who knows where your buttons are, whether they push them on purpose or not, benefits from that.

So let's take the lever away from them, one piece at a time.

The first thing to understand is that a lot of your anger was never about the person in front of you. In the early nineteen seventies, the psychologist Dolf Zillmann studied something he called excitation transfer. He had people do hard physical exercise, and shortly afterwards someone provoked them. Their fight-or-flight response was still switched on from the exercise, and that leftover arousal got poured straight into the provocation. They retaliated harder than people who had been resting.

Your body works the same way at work and at home. You sit in traffic for forty minutes. You read one irritating email. Your heart is still running fast when a colleague makes a small comment in a meeting, and suddenly that comment feels like an attack. The comment did not earn that reaction. It just happened to arrive while your system was already charged.
